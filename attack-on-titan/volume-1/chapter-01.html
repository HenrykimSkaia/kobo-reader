Volume 1 · Chapter 1

The Boy Inside the Cage

A literary condensed recap

Eren woke beneath the tree with the uncomfortable certainty that he had been crying.

The afternoon was mild. Leaves shifted above him in the wind, scattering pieces of sunlight across his face, and for several seconds nothing in the world seemed capable of explaining the grief that had followed him out of sleep. He remembered no clear image from the dream. There was only a sense of distance, as though something precious had already been taken from him and he had awakened too late to stop it.

Mikasa was nearby. She watched him in the quiet way she watched most things—without wasting words, but missing almost nothing. When Eren tried to dismiss the tears, she did not argue. Together they started back toward Shiganshina.

The district lay beneath the enormous shadow of Wall Maria. Houses crowded narrow streets; merchants leaned from open stalls; children ran errands while soldiers at the gates complained about boredom. Above everything rose the wall, so vast that it appeared less like something built by people than a feature of the earth itself.

To the adults of Shiganshina, the wall was peace made visible.

To Eren, it was proof that humanity had surrendered.

He had been born inside it. Everyone he knew had been born inside it. Generations had lived and died without seeing what lay beyond it, and most people seemed satisfied with that bargain. Safety in exchange for the world.

Eren could never understand how they accepted it so easily.

• • •

That afternoon the Survey Corps returned through the gate.

The change in the street began before the soldiers were visible. Conversations softened. People moved aside. Mothers searched the approaching column for sons and daughters. Children rose on their toes, hoping to see heroes.

What entered Shiganshina did not look heroic.

The surviving horses walked slowly. Uniforms were torn. Faces were gray with exhaustion. Empty spaces in the formation were more noticeable than the soldiers who remained. Even those who had returned seemed to carry something of the world outside with them—mud, blood, silence, and the knowledge that others had not made it back.

Eren stared.

He knew what the people around him were thinking. Another expedition wasted. More lives traded for nothing. Another reason never to leave the walls.

But Eren saw something else. Those soldiers had crossed the gate. However badly the journey had ended, they had gone where everyone else was afraid to go.

Failure did not make the outside world meaningless.

If anything, the sight made his longing sharper.

He wanted to know what existed beyond the horizon that the wall stole from him.

• • •

Armin understood.

He was physically smaller than many boys their age and an easy target for children who mistook cruelty for strength. Yet he possessed something Eren valued more than toughness: curiosity. Armin had seen descriptions of places beyond the territory controlled by humanity—vast bodies of water, lands of ice, deserts and mountains unlike anything visible from Shiganshina.

When he spoke about them, his voice changed.

The world became larger.

Eren could almost imagine standing at the edge of an ocean, unable to see the other side. The thought made the walls feel closer around him.

Other children did not share their fascination. To question the walls was to question the order of life itself, and that made Eren and Armin strange. When a group of boys cornered Armin, Eren rushed in without calculating whether he could win. He rarely calculated such things.

Mikasa arrived before courage could become disaster.

She did not need to fight. Her presence was usually enough.

Afterward, Armin spoke of the outside again, and Eren listened with the fierce concentration of someone hearing about a home he had never visited.

• • •

At dinner, the argument finally reached his family.

Carla had heard that Eren wanted to join the Survey Corps. Fear entered her face before anger did. She had seen the returning soldiers too. To her, the Corps was not a symbol of freedom; it was a procession of families learning who would not come home.

Eren insisted that living safely inside the walls was not enough.

The room tightened around the disagreement.

Grisha, Eren's father, did not respond with the same alarm. He was preparing to leave Shiganshina for work and listened with the distracted patience of a man carrying several thoughts at once. Before departing, however, he gave Eren something more powerful than permission.

He gave him a mystery.

One day, Grisha suggested, Eren would be allowed to see what was kept in the locked basement beneath their house.

The promise lodged immediately in Eren's imagination. His father had always forbidden him from entering that room. If the basement held answers, Eren intended to find them.

For the moment, though, evening settled over Shiganshina exactly as it had countless times before.

Shopkeepers closed shutters. Smoke rose from chimneys. Guards leaned against stone and joked with one another. Families prepared meals beneath a sky fading slowly from gold to blue.

The wall stood above them, unbroken.

For a hundred years it had taught the people inside it to confuse repetition with permanence.

Eren looked toward it and thought of the world beyond.

He did not know that the age of waiting was almost over.
