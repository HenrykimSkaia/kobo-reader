미세플라스틱을 '먹는' 플라스틱을 만든 18살 소녀

2026-09-30 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 문제 발견자에서 문제 해결자로

Microplastics are everywhere. These tiny plastic fragments — smaller than 5 millimeters — float in oceans, hide in soil, drift through the air we breathe, and even show up in the food we eat. Because they are so small, they are nearly impossible to remove once they escape into the environment.

Now, an 18-year-old from Ireland has come up with a clever way to fight them. Arya Satheesh invented Eco Purge, a plant-based plastic that breaks down naturally on its own — and while it does, it also helps break down microplastics already polluting the environment. Her invention won the European regional title at The Earth Prize in May 2026, along with $12,500 and access to experts who can help develop the project.

Arya's journey began in 2024, when she built a water-quality sensor for a national science competition. Her device could detect microplastics in water — but it could not remove them. That gap bothered her. Detecting pollution felt like only half a job.

Determined to fix the real problem, she started developing Eco Purge. The bioplastic needed two natural enzymes — special proteins that can chew through plastic molecules. Arya contacted several biotechnology companies for samples, but they were too expensive for a high school student. She did not give up. She reached out to Professor Kevin O'Connor, a researcher at BiOrbic in Ireland. He obtained the enzyme samples for her and helped her gain access to a laboratory at a local university.

By early 2026, Arya had her first prototype. She placed a measured amount of microplastic together with Eco Purge in three environments — soil, salt water, and fresh water — and observed them for six weeks. At the end of the trial, both the microplastic and the Eco Purge itself had decomposed in every sample.

"Science isn't just about understanding the world," she reflected. "It's about leaving it better than we found it."

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 플라스틱을 씹어 먹는 효소

To understand why Eco Purge matters, start with the enemy: plastic is built to last. A plastic bottle is made of long chains of molecules called polymers, linked together like the cars of a very long train. Sunlight, waves, and grinding rocks can shatter the train into smaller pieces — that is how microplastics are born — but nothing in the natural world was equipped to take the train apart car by car. So the pieces pile up: in the stomachs of seabirds, in the deep ocean, in drinking water, and even in human bodies.

This is where enzymes come in. An enzyme is a protein that works like a pair of molecular scissors, each one shaped to cut a specific chemical bond. For most of history, no enzyme on Earth could cut plastic — plastic is a human invention barely a century old. But in 2016, scientists in Japan found bacteria at a recycling site that had evolved an enzyme able to break down PET, plastic in water bottles.

Arya's idea takes this one step further. She wove the enzymes into a new bioplastic itself. Eco Purge is made from plant-based materials rather than petroleum, so it returns to nature when its job is done — like a paper bag melting into the soil. While it decomposes, the embedded enzymes attack the plastic chains of nearby microplastics, snipping the long trains into fragments until microbes can digest the pieces completely.

Her test was simple but honest. Rather than testing in one easy condition, she tried three worlds at once: soil, salty ocean-like water, and fresh water. An invention that only worked in a warm laboratory dish would be a lab trick, not a solution. Watching both the Eco Purge and the microplastics disappear across six weeks in all three environments was the signal that the idea could be real.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 실험실을 벗어나려면

Eco Purge is not yet saving the oceans. The enzymes she uses only work on certain types of plastic — and the plastic world is enormous, from drink bottles to clothing fibers. High temperatures can damage the enzymes, a problem in hot climates. And producing large amounts of enzymes could be expensive. But this is how science moves forward: one prototype at a time. Her Earth Prize award gives her access to mentors, and her next steps will include testing more kinds of plastic, searching for heat-resistant enzymes, and finding cheaper ways to manufacture everything.

The bigger lesson reaches beyond one teenager and one invention. Around the world, researchers are hunting for plastic-eating microbes in landfills, compost heaps, and even the guts of waxworms — tiny creatures that can chew through shopping bags. Engineers are designing washing-machine filters that catch microfibers before they reach rivers. Policy makers are banning single-use plastics city by city.

Eco Purge adds a new idea to that toolbox: packaging that cleans up after itself and its neighbors. Imagine fishing nets that vanish if lost at sea, or food wrappers that leave the beach cleaner than they found it. That future is still distant, but Arya's prototype proves the first step is possible. And she started, remember, not with a factory or a fortune, but with an email to a professor willing to help a curious student.
