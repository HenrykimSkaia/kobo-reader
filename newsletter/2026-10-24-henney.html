Source: I-Journey — Sydney's Second Airport Finally Opens, and It Never Closes

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 내일, 시드니 서쪽 하늘에 새 공항이 열린다

Tomorrow, Sunday, October 25, 2026, at 11 in the morning Sydney time, something will happen that Sydney has waited decades to see. A Jetstar Airbus A320, flight JQ362, will lift off from a brand-new airport and head for the Gold Coast. It will be the first passenger flight ever to leave Western Sydney International Airport.

The airport sits at Badgerys Creek, about 44 kilometers west of central Sydney, and its code is WSI. Its full name is Western Sydney International (Nancy-Bird Walton) Airport, named after one of Australia’s greatest pilots — more about her in Part 2. Jetstar will also fly to Melbourne and Brisbane from day one, and the Gold Coast route is already busier than planned: first announced as four flights a week in June, it was raised to a daily service in September because so many people wanted seats.

The rollout continues quickly. Air New Zealand will fly to Auckland three times a week starting Monday, October 26, the airport’s first international service. Singapore Airlines plans a daily flight to Singapore from November 23, and Qantas is scheduled to add Melbourne and Brisbane services from March 28, 2027. Cargo is already moving: the airport’s freight precinct opened in July 2026, so tomorrow’s passenger flights are the last big piece of the opening, not the first.

Why the Gold Coast first? It is one of Australia’s busiest domestic air routes, a holiday corridor between New South Wales and Queensland. A first flight is never just a flight. It is a promise, made in public, that the whole system works.

That promise took a long time to keep. Sydney argued about a second airport for decades, picked the Badgerys Creek site, and started building in 2018. Major construction finished in June 2025, and then came a full year of testing and trial operations — the rehearsals before the curtain rises. Tomorrow, the rehearsal ends.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 공항은 활주로가 아니라 시스템이다

When you picture an airport, you probably picture a runway. But a runway alone is not an airport, and Sydney’s story proves it. The city’s older airport, Kingsford Smith, has a strict overnight curfew: no scheduled flights between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. It also caps aircraft movements at 80 per hour. Sydney had simply outgrown those limits.

Western Sydney International was designed around the opposite idea: no curfew at all. Planes can arrive and depart around the clock, which matters most for freight and for airlines that want to fly at night. Stage one has a single 3.7-kilometer runway and one integrated terminal for domestic and international passengers, built to handle up to 10 million passengers a year. Later stages could add a second runway and a larger terminal, growing toward 82 million passengers a year by 2063.

But an airport is also everything around the runway. Getting there is part of the system, and here the opening has a gap a sharp reader should notice: the new Sydney Metro rail line to the airport is not due until 2027. For now, travelers will rely on the toll-free M12 motorway, buses running every 30 minutes, a free shuttle from St Marys station, and more than 6,000 parking spaces. A beautiful terminal with a painful journey to reach it is still a broken system.

Now, the name on the building. Nancy-Bird Walton grew up loving airplanes, saved her wages for flying lessons, and trained with the pioneer Charles Kingsford Smith. While still a teenager, she became the first woman in Australia to hold a commercial pilot’s licence. In the 1930s she flew an air ambulance service for the Far West Children’s Health Scheme, carrying nurses and sick children across the outback — work that earned her the nickname “Angel of the Outback.” In 1950 she founded the Australian Women Pilots’ Association. Naming the airport after a pilot who had to fight for the right to fly at all is a message: this place belongs to anyone with the skill and the stubbornness to earn it.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 24시간 공항이 바꿀 것들, 그리고 남은 질문

So what changes when an airport never sleeps? First, think about the map. About three million people live in Western Sydney, and until now most of them had to travel east across the whole city to catch a flight. A closer airport means shorter journeys, more choice, and new jobs — pilots and cabin crew, but also baggage handlers, engineers, and freight crews. Night flights also free up daytime space at the older airport, because cargo planes that once waited for a daytime slot can now fly when the city is asleep.

Second, think about the trade-off, because every gain in aviation has a price. An airport without a curfew is wonderful for airlines and terrible for light sleepers nearby. Noise at 3 a.m. is a real cost, paid by real neighbors, and how the airport handles complaints and night noise rules will decide whether the community sees WSI as a good neighbor or a noisy one. A future pilot should learn this early: flying is not only about machines and weather. It is about sharing the sky with people on the ground.

Third, watch the first weeks, not just the first flight. New airports almost always stumble at first. The question is how fast the team fixes problems. If the metro line arrives on time in 2027, if airlines keep adding routes, and if passenger numbers climb toward that 10-million target, WSI will look like a triumph of patience. If growth stalls, the second runway may wait a very long time.

For a future pilot, the timeline is personal again. The teenager reading this today could be flying from WSI within a decade — perhaps on a Gold Coast route like JQ362, perhaps on a night freight run that only exists because this airport has no curfew. Aviation’s biggest breakthroughs are sometimes not new airplanes at all, but new places for airplanes to go, and new rules about when they may fly.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are the captain of flight JQ362, the very first passenger flight out of Western Sydney International Airport tomorrow morning. The cabin is full, the cameras are waiting outside, and before takeoff you must make the announcement passengers will remember. Everyone on board knows this flight is historic, and everyone is listening.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: one thing you would want to check before you speak (for example, what the airport’s name stands for, or why this airport can fly at night when Sydney’s older airport cannot) and why that detail matters; how you would explain the moment to passengers in your own words; and what it would mean for the airport if your announcement makes the whole cabin feel proud rather than just nervous.
