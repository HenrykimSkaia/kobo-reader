물만 내뿜는 80석 비행기: 에어버스 수소 여객기 ZEROe, 2040년대 데뷔로 목표 수정

2026-10-23 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: Aerospace America — Airbus scales back hydrogen ambitions, aiming for 80-seat aircraft

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 에어버스가 수소 비행기 계획을 바꿨다

Last week, in Toulouse, France — the city where Airbus builds its biggest jets — the company changed the plan for the most radical airplane it has ever designed. Lars Wagner, the head of Airbus’s commercial aircraft business, told reporters that the company’s first hydrogen airplane will be smaller and later than promised: a propeller-driven regional plane with about 80 seats, powered by fuel cells, arriving sometime in the 2040s.

The program is called ZEROe, and it began in late 2020 with a bold promise: a large hydrogen passenger jet by 2035. But last year Airbus admitted the truth — neither the technology nor the airports were ready in time. The most recent concept, a 100-seat fuel-cell design, has now been scaled back again to around 80 seats, with a range of about 1,000 nautical miles — roughly 1,850 kilometers, enough for key European routes such as Paris to Rome.

“Eighty is our entry into the zero-emission market,” Wagner said, “and the beauty of fuel cell technology is it is capable of scaling up to a bigger size.” In other words: start small, prove it works, then grow.

The plan has clear steps. First, Airbus will flight-test a 20-seat demonstrator aircraft. Only then will the design grow to 80 seats. In July, Airbus announced a joint venture with the German engine maker MTU Aero Engines to develop a fully electric hydrogen fuel-cell engine — a striking move, because it is the first time Airbus itself is becoming an engine manufacturer. The joint venture is expected to be up and running by next year.

Wagner also revealed the politics behind the decision: the ZEROe will stay a regional airplane so it does not compete with Airbus’s own next-generation single-aisle jet, due to enter service in the second half of the 2030s. “I need to be aware that I don’t create competition internally,” he said — so the future hydrogen plane and the future conventional jet will fly side by side in the 2040s.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 연료전지로 나는 법, 그리고 수소의 무게

You have read about electric airplanes before — the battery-powered ALIA, the hybrid Electra. Their problem is simple: batteries are heavy. To fly farther, you need more batteries; more batteries mean more weight; more weight needs still more power. Hydrogen offers a way out — not as a battery, but as fuel for an onboard power plant called a fuel cell.

A fuel cell is like a battery that never runs out as long as you feed it. It takes hydrogen from a tank, mixes it with oxygen from the air, and — through a chemical reaction, with no flame at all — makes electricity. The only exhaust is water. Imagine a car engine that drips pure water from its tailpipe: that is the promise.

But Airbus made a deliberate choice here, and it matters. You could also simply burn hydrogen in an engine, the way jets burn kerosene today. That would be easier — but burning creates nitrogen oxides, the pollutants in smog, and throws extra water vapor high into the sky, where it forms contrails, those white lines behind jets. Contrails trap heat, so a hydrogen-burning plane would still warm the planet. Fuel cells skip the flame entirely, which is why Airbus chose them for its “zero-emission” claim.

Then comes the hard part: carrying the hydrogen. Liquid hydrogen packs enormous energy for its weight, but it is fluffy: it needs about four times as much space as kerosene for the same energy. Worse, it must stay colder than almost anything in nature, around minus 253 degrees Celsius, or it boils away. Picture fueling your plane from a giant thermos bottle the size of a school bus. Designing tanks, pipes, and wings around that is the engineers’ real puzzle.

And there is the chicken-and-egg problem: no airport today has hydrogen pipelines or storage tanks. “We need to obviously see hydrogen coming up as an infrastructure,” Wagner said. The airplane is only half the invention; the other half is the fuel station.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 2040년대 조종석을 향한 세 가지 관문

So what happens between now and the 2040s? The roadmap has three waypoints. First, the 20-seat demonstrator must prove that a fuel-cell airplane can fly safely — take off, climb, cruise, land. Only then does the design grow to 80 seats. And because fuel cells keep improving, Wagner insists the design can keep growing after that: regional today, bigger tomorrow. Airbus deliberately keeps this plane small enough not to fight its own next-generation single-aisle jet, planned for the late 2030s. The 2040s will have two Airbus futures flying in parallel.

Second, someone must build the invisible half: the infrastructure. Hydrogen has to be produced, liquefied, delivered to airports, and stored at minus 253 degrees — a supply chain that barely exists today. And here is the question a sharp reader should ask: where does the hydrogen come from? If it is made from natural gas, the carbon is just moved somewhere else. Only “green” hydrogen — split from water using clean electricity — makes the plane truly clean. The fuel’s color matters as much as the plane’s design.

Third, there is a race. China’s latest five-year plan puts explicit emphasis on hydrogen development, and Airbus executives openly say they are watching. “We know from other industries that what China puts on their five-year plan, they execute,” Wagner said. The MTU joint venture is partly about “European sovereignty” — Europe building its own hydrogen engines rather than buying the future from someone else. This is no longer just engineering; it is geopolitics with wings.

For a future pilot, the timeline is personal. The 2040s are not science fiction — you will be in your early thirties, exactly the age of a working airline captain. The cockpit you train for may have propellers again, fueling checklists written for super-cold tanks, and passengers asking, “Is hydrogen safe?” How you would answer that question is something you can start practicing today.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine it is 2048 and you are the captain of the very first ZEROe flight with 80 passengers on board. During boarding, a nervous passenger asks you two questions: “Aren’t propellers old-fashioned?” and “Isn’t hydrogen dangerous?” The cabin door is about to close, and everyone is listening for your answer.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: one thing about the hydrogen fuel you would want to check before answering (for example, how it was made, or how it is stored) and why that detail matters; how you would explain that propellers are a choice, not a step backward; and what it would mean for aviation if your answer convinces the whole cabin.
