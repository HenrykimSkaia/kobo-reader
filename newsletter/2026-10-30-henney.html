국수를 먹고 자란 고양이: 100년 만의 새 야생 고양이 틸카요

2026-10-30 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews — Scientists Identify First New Wild Cat Species In Over 100 Years

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 숲에서 온 낯선 고양이

In the cloud forests of Bolivia, on the eastern slopes of the Andes Mountains, people have told stories for generations about a small spotted cat that hunts in the shadows. They call it the tilcayo. Scientists had never given it a name of their own — until September 17, 2026. On that day, a study published in the scientific journal Current Biology announced that the tilcayo is a species entirely new to science: Leopardus tilcayo. It is the first new living wild cat species identified anywhere in the world in more than one hundred years. The last one, the Uruguayan pampas cat, was classified as a distinct species in 1923.

The tilcayo is small, even for a wild cat. It measures about 18 inches (46 centimeters) from the head to the base of its tail and weighs about 3 pounds (1.4 kilograms) — roughly the size of a house cat, but leaner, wilder, and built for the forest. Its light brown fur is covered with large, irregular dark spots, like ink blots that never quite dried. It lives in the Yungas, a band of misty mountain forest where the Andes drop toward the Amazon lowlands, one of the most living-packed places on Earth.

The road to the discovery began ten years earlier, in 2016, with a case of mistaken identity. A local resident found a tiny spotted male cat beside a road and, believing it was an ordinary house cat, took it home. For about a year, the family fed it noodles, rice, and eggs. Slowly, though, it became clear that this was no house cat. The animal was wild, and it needed experts. The family brought it to the Senda Verde wildlife sanctuary, where it was given a name: Tigrino.

In 2017, a Bolivian biologist named Paola Nogales-Ascarrunz visited the sanctuary to examine what the staff called a ‘weird cat.’ She photographed Tigrino and concluded that he was a northern tiger cat, a known member of the Leopardus group of small American wild cats. The case seemed closed. She went home, and Tigrino stayed at the sanctuary, the only cat of his kind anyone had ever seen.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 점박이 무늬가 말해주는 것

Two years later, the case reopened — because Paola looked twice. In 2019, while she was preparing a field guide to Bolivia’s wild cats, she opened her old photographs of Tigrino. This time something bothered her. The dark spots on his fur were much larger than the spots of a northern tiger cat. A field guide demands precision: if you draw the wrong spots, readers will misidentify every cat they meet. So Paola and her team decided to test the question properly, with DNA.

DNA is a kind of instruction book carried in every cell, and comparing instruction books can settle family arguments that eyes cannot. The team compared Tigrino’s DNA with the DNA of 38 other cats from the Leopardus group — ocelots, margays, oncillas, and their relatives. The result was clear. Tigrino did not belong to any known species. His family line had split from its closest relatives about 1.4 million years ago and had been evolving on its own ever since. A million and a half years of separate history, hiding in a spotted coat.

That long separation also explains the spots. In the Yungas, sunlight filters through leaves in broken patches, and a coat of large, uneven blotches breaks up the cat’s outline the way camouflage breaks up a soldier’s silhouette. Each Leopardus species wears a slightly different pattern, tuned by evolution to its own forest. To a casual glance, the tilcayo looks like its cousins. To a trained eye — or a DNA test — it is unmistakably itself.

There is one more detail worth noticing. The scientists did not invent a new name. They chose Leopardus tilcayo, keeping the name local communities had used for generations. Science often arrives late to knowledge that people living in a place have held all along. By keeping the old name, the study gave credit where it was due: the forest’s neighbors knew the tilcayo long before a journal did.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 틸카요를 지키는 일

So what happens next? First, the search. Tigrino is still the only known living member of his species. Paola and her team are now setting camera traps — motion-triggered cameras strapped to trees — across the Yungas, hoping to photograph more tilcayos in the wild. Every new photograph would answer a question: How many are there? Where exactly do they hunt? Do they roam alone, like most small cats, or tolerate neighbors? A species cannot be protected until scientists know where it lives and what it needs.

Second, the clock. The Yungas forests face fires, deforestation, and mining. A forest that loses its trees loses far more than trees: it loses the shade, the prey, and the quiet that a small hunter needs. The tilcayo survived 1.4 million years of change, but it cannot survive the loss of its home. Finding the cat in time — while the forest still stands — is the whole point of the camera traps.

And third, there is a lesson that has nothing to do with cats. In 2026, on a planet mapped by satellites and photographed from orbit, a brand-new mammal species was hiding in plain sight, eating noodles in someone’s kitchen. It stayed hidden not because it was far away, but because everyone who saw it assumed they already knew what it was. Paola’s discovery did not begin with an expedition. It began with a second look at an old photograph. Curiosity, it turns out, is a research tool. The next species new to science might be sitting in a photograph you already took — waiting for someone patient enough to look again.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are a young biologist in the Yungas, checking the camera traps you set last week. One camera caught a clear photo of a second tilcayo — the first proof that Tigrino is not alone in the world. You have one satellite phone call to tell Paola the news before the batteries die.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: one thing you would want to find out next (for example, whether the new cat is male or female, or where exactly it hunts) and why that detail matters; how you would describe the photograph so Paola believes you; and what it would mean for the tilcayo if the forest around the camera trap keeps shrinking.
