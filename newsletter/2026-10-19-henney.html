49년의 비행: 보이저 1호가 '빛 하루' 거리에 도달하다

2026-10-19 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews — NASA's Voyager 1 Spacecraft Approaches Deep Space Milestone

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 49년째 비행 중인 보이저 1호

On November 18, 2026 — less than a month from now — the most distant traveler in human history will cross a finish line no machine has ever reached. At about 5:16 in the morning Eastern Time, Voyager 1 will sit exactly one light-day away from Earth: about 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers. A light-day is the distance that light itself needs a full 24 hours to cross.

Think about that. Light is the fastest thing in the universe — in one second it could circle the Earth more than seven times. And yet a radio message from NASA, racing at that same unbeatable speed, would take an entire day to reach the spacecraft. If engineers send a command on the morning of November 18, the answer will not arrive until two days later. Voyager 1’s own voice is nearly gone by the time it reaches home. Its transmitter sends out only about 23 watts of power — roughly the same as a refrigerator light bulb. Catching that signal from 16 billion miles away with giant dish antennas is like hearing a whisper from across a continent.

Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977, on what was supposed to be a five-year mission: a close-up study of Jupiter and Saturn, the two largest planets in our solar system. It delivered spectacularly, photographing their rings, moons, and atmospheres as no one had ever seen them. Then, because the spacecraft was still healthy, NASA simply kept flying it — outward, into the dark. In 2012 it crossed the heliopause, the invisible boundary where the Sun’s influence ends, becoming the first human-made object to sail into interstellar space, the true ocean between the stars. It has now been traveling for 49 years. It took 49 years, 2 months, and 11 days to cover the distance that light crosses in a single day.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 중력 새총과 죽어가는 배터리

How does a machine built in the 1970s — with less computing power than the phone in your pocket — fly straight for half a century without running out of fuel or getting lost? The answer is one of the cleverest tricks in space history: the gravity assist.

Voyager 1 did not just burn fuel and aim at the stars. As it flew past Jupiter, it fell into the planet’s gravity and was flung outward, faster than it arrived — like a stone gaining speed from a slingshot. The same trick worked at Saturn. This was only possible because of a rare lineup of the outer planets, a Grand Tour alignment that happens once every 176 years. NASA’s engineers waited for the right moment and let the planets themselves do the pushing. That is why Voyager 1 travels at 38,000 miles per hour today without spending any fuel to stay on course.

Power was the harder problem. A spacecraft 16 billion miles from the Sun cannot use solar panels; sunlight there is weaker than candlelight. So Voyager 1 carries a nuclear battery called an RTG, which turns the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity. But nothing lasts forever. The battery makes a little less power every year, like a phone battery slowly dying — except this phone can never be recharged. To keep the mission alive, engineers have been switching off the spacecraft’s instruments one by one. In April 2026, they turned off the Low-Energy Charged Particles instrument, which had been studying how space weather changes far from the Sun. Only two science instruments are still awake.

And then there is the strangeness of the place it swims through. Interstellar space is not completely empty; it is a thin soup of gas and magnetic fields left behind by ancient stars. Voyager 1’s remaining instruments measure that magnetic field and the faint vibrations of plasma — the ghost of distant starlight. The readings surprised scientists: the Sun’s magnetic “bubble,” the heliosphere, is not a neat round balloon. It may be shaped more like a croissant, squashed on one side. One small probe, dying slowly, is drawing the map of our home.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 침묵 이후의 4만 년

What happens next? Honestly, no one knows exactly how long Voyager 1 can keep talking. If engineers manage its fading power carefully, the last instrument may keep sending data into the early 2030s. Then the nuclear battery will be too weak to warm even a single circuit, and the spacecraft will go silent — the way a candle goes out, quietly, with no one there to see it.

But silence is not the end. Voyager 1 will keep drifting at 38,000 miles per hour long after its last whisper. In about 300 years it will reach the Oort Cloud, a shell of icy comets at the true outer edge of the Sun’s family. In about 40,000 years — longer than all of human civilization — it will drift within about 1.6 light-years of a small star called Gliese 445. No one will be alive who remembers the launch. No one will know the engineers’ names. And yet the machine will still be flying straight.

It will not be empty. Bolted to Voyager 1 is the Golden Record: a 12-inch gold-plated disc carrying sounds and pictures of Earth — waves crashing, a baby crying, music from around the world, greetings in dozens of languages. It was built for a reader that may never exist. Carl Sagan, the astronomer who led the project, called it a bottle tossed into the cosmic ocean.

Questions remain unanswered. What is the exact shape of the heliosphere — bubble, croissant, or something stranger? What does the magnetic field of interstellar space really look like? Every extra year of data from Voyager 1 helps. The lesson is bigger than space: build things that last, aim them at the right moment, and let them teach you long after you stop being able to fix them. Voyager 1 was designed to survive five years. It has survived 49. Sometimes the most powerful technology is simply patience.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine it is 2035. You are the newest engineer on the Voyager team at NASA. The spacecraft’s power has dropped so low that only one instrument can stay on — and the team must vote tonight on which one survives.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: one thing you would look up to help you decide (for example, what each remaining instrument actually measures); why the instrument you choose matters more than the other; and what it would mean if the surviving instrument sent back something no one expected.
