새의 깃털을 단 비행기: 실속을 이기는 날개

2026-10-05 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: Science News Explores

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 갈매기 날개를 베낀 비행기 날개

Next time you watch a brown pelican glide, look closely at the top of its wings. You will see rows of small, overlapping feathers rising from near the bird’s shoulders like shingles on a roof. These are called covert feathers, and for millions of years they have quietly helped birds pull off flying tricks that human engineers still struggle to copy: steep, slow landings, sharp turns, and flying through gusty air without falling out of the sky.

Now a team of engineers has borrowed that trick. Led by Aimy Wissa, an engineer at Princeton University, the researchers lined model airplane wings with rows of tiny, lightweight flaps that imitate covert feathers. Then they tested the wings in a wind tunnel — a laboratory tunnel where engineers blow air over model wings and watch how it flows. The results were striking. Airfoils fitted with five rows of the feather-like flaps produced up to 45 percent more lift, the upward force that holds a plane in the air. At the same time, drag — the air resistance that tries to slow a plane down — fell by 31 percent.

Here is the clever part: these flaps need no motors and no pilot commands. They simply fan out on their own whenever the wing meets oncoming air at a steep angle, a situation engineers call a high angle of attack. That is exactly when a normal wing is most likely to “stall,” or suddenly lose lift. The findings appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, and follow-up tests with remote-controlled aircraft showed the flaps really do reduce stall and make flight steadier in real air.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 양력과 항력, 그리고 실속

To understand why this matters, picture the two forces that decide every flight: lift and drag. An airplane wing — engineers call the curved shape an airfoil — is shaped so that air flows smoothly around it, leaving a zone of low pressure above the wing. That low pressure pulls the wing upward: that is lift. At the same time, the wing pushes air aside, and the air pushes back. That pushback is drag.

The trouble starts at a high angle of attack. Imagine holding a paper airplane with its nose tipped sharply upward before you throw it. When a wing meets the air at a steep angle like that, the smooth flow over the top of the wing can break away, tumbling into swirls. The low-pressure zone vanishes, lift collapses, and the wing stalls. Every student pilot in the world trains to recognize and recover from stalls, because in a real airplane, losing lift at low speed — for example, during landing — is dangerous.

The bird-inspired flaps fight stall in two ways. Flaps near the front of the wing act like the bristles of a comb, smoothing the air so it hugs the wing’s surface instead of breaking away — that is why lift rises and drag falls. A single row near the rear of the wing does something different: it works like a dam, stopping high-pressure air near the wing’s trailing edge from creeping forward. That matters because, as Wissa explains, the front of the wing needs low pressure to produce lift. The more rows of flaps the team added, the stronger the effect, with five rows delivering the headline numbers.

Try a thought experiment you can do in a moving car: with an adult driving, hold your hand flat out the window, palm down, fingers together. Tip your hand so the front edge rises. Feel how the air lifts your hand — and how, past a certain angle, the smooth lift turns into shaking turbulence. These little flaps are designed to push that shaking point further away, so the wing keeps flying where it would otherwise give up.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 실험실에서 하늘로, 그리고 남은 질문들

Wind tunnels are honest, but they are not the sky. So the team took the idea outside, fitting the feather-like flaps to remote-controlled aircraft. The small planes flew through gusts and slow, steep approaches — the exact moments when stalls love to strike — and the flaps held their own: stalls became rarer, and the flights steadier. Wissa says the flaps could be especially valuable during landings on very short runways, where a plane must fly slow and steep. “Without the flaps, you might not be able to safely complete such maneuvers,” she says. “But now, you can.”

That points to a bigger prize. Less drag means an airplane burns less fuel to fly the same route, and fuel is one of an airline’s largest expenses. A wing that lifts more and drags less could also let planes fly safely in rougher weather and land at smaller airports — the kind of airports that connect remote communities in Canada’s North.

But plenty of questions remain open. Feathers are replaced constantly by living birds; airplane parts must survive rain, ice, and years of sun with little maintenance. Engineers will need flaps that are light, tough, and cheap to inspect and replace. Aviation regulators — the people who certify that every new airplane part is safe — will want years of testing before these wings carry passengers. And nobody yet knows the best layout: five rows worked in the lab, but the ideal number, size, and spacing on a real airliner’s wing are still to be found.

That unfinished puzzle is the exciting part. The paper’s lead author G. Sedky and colleagues proved that copying birds can expand what airplanes can safely do. The next steps — materials, testing, certification — are the kind of problems engineers, and perhaps pilots, will chew on for a decade. And it all started with someone watching a pelican’s shoulders and asking: what if wings worked a little more like feathers?
