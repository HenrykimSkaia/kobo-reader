전설의 파일럿을 찾아서: 아멜리아 에어하트와 타라이아 물체

2026-10-06 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: National Geographic

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 전설의 파일럿, 90년 만의 재탐사

On October 13 — just one week from today — a small boat will sail toward one of the loneliest places on Earth, chasing a mystery that is nearly ninety years old. A 17-person team, backed by Purdue University and a nonprofit called the Archaeological Legacy Institute, will land on Nikumaroro Island, a tiny coral atoll in the Pacific nation of Kiribati. Their mission: to find the lost airplane of Amelia Earhart, the most famous woman pilot in history.

Earhart was the superstar of the sky: in 1932 she became the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic Ocean. Purdue later recruited her as a visiting professor, where she urged young women toward science and aviation careers. With $47,000 from a special university fund, she bought a Lockheed Electra 10-E, a sleek twin-engine airplane she called her "flying laboratory" — built for ultra-long flights, with extra fuel tanks and a radio direction finder.

In 1937 she attempted what no one had ever done: fly around the world along the equator, with navigator Fred Noonan beside her. On July 2 they took off from Lae, New Guinea, bound for Howland Island, a speck of land in the middle of the Pacific. Earhart called the island "a fantastically tiny target" — just one square mile of land lost in the vast ocean.

Her radio calls grew desperate. "We must be on you but cannot see you ... gas is running low," she told the crew of the U.S. Coast Guard cutter waiting near Howland. An hour later came her last confirmed words: "We are on the line 157-337 ... We are running north and south." Then silence. Neither Earhart nor Noonan was ever seen again, and the Electra vanished with them.

Now Purdue — which has preserved Earhart's papers for decades but never joined a search — has decided this one is different. The university is offering the expedition a $500,000 line of credit and sending two alumni who have flown in space as astronauts. If all goes as planned, the team will spend five days examining a lagoon where a satellite image shows something that looks like a plane.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 타라이아 물체 — 증거와 탐지 기술

The search is built on a theory with decades of detective work behind it. A group called TIGHAR has run 14 expeditions to the atoll over thirty years. They argue that Earhart and Noonan followed that navigational line southeast, spotted Nikumaroro, and landed on its broad, flat reef. Later, the rising tide may have dragged the battered plane off the reef into the ocean, leaving the fliers stranded as castaways.

The clues read like a treasure-hunter's notebook. Islanders recalled seeing airplane wreckage on the reef. Searchers found bits of shoes, including a woman's shoe from the same brand Earhart wore, plus 1930s-era artifacts at a campsite — a zipper pull, a jackknife, a compact mirror. Old records show four radio signals picked up on Wake, Guam, and Hawai'i shortly after the disappearance that pointed near Nikumaroro.

Then, in 2020, an amateur researcher named Michael Ashmore was browsing Apple Maps when he spotted something strange in the aquamarine water off the lagoon's Taraia Point: a black circle with lines stretching out, like an airplane seen from above. He called it the Taraia Object.

TIGHAR's experts were skeptical. To them it looked like a submerged palm tree and its root ball. Expedition leader Richard Pettigrew was more open-minded. He found the object first appeared in images from April 27, 2015 — right after Tropical Cyclone Pam had battered the island, which could have swept sediment off something metallic hiding underneath.

Not everyone is convinced. A scaling study suggests the object is too small to be the Electra — searchers should look for a cross-shaped object at least 22 feet wide. Ashmore himself now stamps his original image "Debunked," calling it a tree. the expedition's answer is simple: look with better tools. They will scan with sonar, which fires sound waves into the water and listens for the echo the way bats navigate. Magnetometers will detect tiny distortions in Earth's magnetic field caused by buried metal. Then a hydro-probe — "a little pipe with water forcing through it" — will pierce the sediment for a hands-on answer, and hydraulic dredging will clear the mud so they can see the object with their own eyes.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 10월 13일, 남은 질문들

The plan is tight. After a six-day sail from Tarawa, the team gets only five days on Nikumaroro: scan first, probe next, dredge last. If the object turns out to be the Electra, they will return next year to excavate it. If it is a tree or a rock, Pettigrew says that is fine too: "My definition of success is that we correctly identify what the Taraia Object is."

That attitude reveals something important about how science works. A mystery is not a failure; it is an invitation. Every Earhart theory must survive the same test: does the evidence fit? The Nikumaroro theory must explain why no one found the plane in eighty years — and the Taraia Object might finally answer that, because cyclones keep rearranging the lagoon floor, burying and uncovering whatever lies there.

Plenty of questions stay open even if the object is just a tree. Why did Earhart's radio fail at the worst possible moment? How could two skilled navigators lose a one-square-mile island? What does it mean that an ordinary person, browsing satellite maps on a phone, could restart the hunt for the century's most famous missing airplane?

Earhart once wrote that "the most difficult thing is the decision to act; the rest is merely tenacity." Now a new generation of explorers — with sonars instead of sextants — is making the same decision. Whether they find a flying laboratory or an old palm tree, they will have solved something. For a kid who dreams of being a pilot, that is the real lesson: the sky keeps its secrets, but it also keeps its invitations.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are one of the 17 explorers sailing six days from Tarawa to Nikumaroro. On the morning of day five, the sonar screen shows a shape in the lagoon. Everyone is watching.

Write 150–200 words in English. Describe the scene: what you see on the screen, what the team does next, and how you feel. Then name one clue you would look for to decide whether the object is the Electra or a tree, and explain why that clue matters. Finally, write what it would mean to you if the object turns out to be just a tree — and what you would do next.
