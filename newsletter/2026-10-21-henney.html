비행기의 심장을 새로 만들다: 캐나다의 신형 터보프롭 엔진 PT9

2026-10-21 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: RTX (PR Newswire) — RTX's Pratt & Whitney Canada introduces certified PT9 engine

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 5년간 비밀리에 만들어진 엔진

On October 21, 2026, in a packed convention hall in Las Vegas, an engine maker pulled off a surprise it had kept quiet for five years. Pratt & Whitney Canada — the company behind the most famous small-airplane engine in history, the PT6 — unveiled a brand-new turboprop engine called the PT9. It was the company's first all-new turboprop in decades, and almost nobody had seen it coming. The earliest public hints had only slipped out last year, buried in dense American regulatory paperwork. By the time the covers came off at the NBAA-BACE business aviation show, the PT9 had already survived more than 14,000 hours of testing on 12 test engines. And it carried one more surprise: it had already been certified by Transport Canada on October 1.

The PT9 is aimed at the 1,700 to 2,300 shaft-horsepower range — roughly the muscle of two thousand small cars pulling together. Compared with today's engines in its class, it produces 33 percent more power at altitude while burning 20 percent less fuel. More strength with less thirst: that combination is the holy grail of engine design, and it is why the announcement made headlines across the aviation world.

Why build it now? Because customers asked for it. "We sized this based on market demand," said Satheeshkumar Kumarasingam, the company's president, "and people who said, 'Hey, we need something in the 2,000-shaft horsepower range.'" The engine will be built in Lethbridge, Alberta — about 200 kilometers southeast of Calgary — at the company's center of excellence for the PT6 family. It bridges the power gap between the smaller PT6 series and the larger PW100 series, and the company stresses it is "not a replacement" for the legendary PT6. Serial production is expected in about 18 months, and although no airplane maker has signed on yet, Kumarasingam says the company is "pretty confident of signing one or two customers."

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 터보프롭은 어떻게 돌아가는가

A turboprop is, at heart, a jet engine wearing a propeller. Inside, air follows four steps engineers memorize as "suck, squeeze, burn, blow." A fan sucks air in; a compressor squeezes it into a tight, high-pressure stream; fuel is sprayed in and burned, exploding the air into a super-hot blast; and that blast rushes backward through turbine wheels, spinning them like a pinwheel in a storm. In a jetliner, most of that blast shoots straight out the back to push the plane forward. In a turboprop, the turbines instead turn a long shaft — through a gearbox — that spins a big propeller at the front. The propeller does most of the pushing.

Why bother with the propeller at all? Because propellers are champions of efficiency at the speeds and altitudes where regional airplanes live. A jet engine is like a sprinter: magnificent at full speed, wasteful at a jog. A propeller is like a marathon runner: steady, efficient, perfect for the short hops between small cities. That is why turboprops rule regional aviation — the Calgary-to-Lethbridge kind of flying — while jets rule the long ocean crossings.

The PT9 squeezes more out of this old idea with new tricks. Thin air at altitude starves engines of oxygen, so "33 percent more power at altitude" means the airplane can climb faster and cruise higher, where the air is thin and smooth — exactly where pilots want to be over the Rockies. Twenty percent less fuel matters twice: fuel is an airline's biggest bill, and every liter unburned is carbon dioxide never released. The engine also carries a digital nervous system: sensors read more than 300 parameters, warning mechanics of trouble before it happens — like a doctor checking your pulse and temperature at once. And like its PT6 E-Series cousins, it uses single-lever control: the pilot pushes one lever instead of juggling three separate ones for power, propeller pitch, and fuel mixture. It is the difference between driving an automatic and a stick shift — simpler, safer, and one less thing to think about in a busy cockpit.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 조용한 혁명의 다음 장

The PT9's cleverest feature may be what it does not do yet. Its modular design is "future-ready" for hybridization — an electric motor can be bolted onto the architecture later, turning the turboprop into a hybrid thermal-electric powerplant. Pratt & Whitney Canada is already running a hybrid experiment: a modified Dash 8 with a 2-megawatt hybrid system, heading for a test cell and then flight next year. In Europe, the company won a project to build a hybrid version of its PW127XT engine for the ATR 72-600 regional airliner, targeting flight tests by 2030. The PT9 is the bridge to that future: clean enough for today, built to evolve for tomorrow.

None of this happens in a vacuum. The PT9 arrives as a direct answer to GE Aerospace's Catalyst, a rival new-generation turboprop — and engine rivalries, like sports rivalries, make both sides better. The real contest, though, is against physics and chemistry: aviation must cut its emissions, and there is no single magic invention waiting in a lab. Progress comes in steps — 20 percent less fuel here, 33 percent more power there — each one compounding across tens of thousands of engines flying millions of hours.

For someone who dreams of becoming a pilot, this story matters in a personal way. Pilots do not just steer airplanes; they manage their hearts. Understanding how a turboprop breathes — why it is strong at altitude, why it sips fuel, how its digital brain watches over it — is the difference between a driver and an aviator. And here is the part closest to home: the engine that may one day power the regional airliners you fly is being built in Lethbridge, Alberta, certified by Transport Canada, about 200 kilometers from your front door. The future of flight is not only happening in Las Vegas convention halls. Some of it is being assembled on the Canadian prairie, not far from where you live.
