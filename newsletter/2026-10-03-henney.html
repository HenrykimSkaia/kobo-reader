5달러로 하늘을 날다: 세계 최대 전기 비행기 X1

2026-10-03 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: NewsForKids

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 5달러로 날아오른 비행기

On the morning of August 12, 2026, an unusual airplane taxied down the runway at Plattsburgh International Airport in upstate New York. From a distance it looked like any small regional airliner: a 30-seat plane with a wingspan of 106 feet — wider than a basketball court is long. But when it took off, witnesses noticed something strange. It was quiet. This was the X1, built by a Swedish company called Heart Aerospace, and on that morning it became the largest battery-electric airplane ever to fly.

The numbers were hard to believe. The X1 weighed more than 25,000 pounds — about as much as a city bus — yet during its entire 27-minute flight it burned no jet fuel at all. The electricity it used cost roughly five dollars, about the price of a sandwich. There were no passengers on board, only the pilot: Anders Forslund, the company’s founder. He climbed to 1,100 feet, roughly the height of the Eiffel Tower, and flew a full test program — takeoff, turns, maneuvers, and a smooth landing — while engineers on the ground watched every reading.

The flight had to answer one enormous question: can an airplane the size of a real airliner fly on batteries alone? For 27 minutes, the answer was yes. The plane’s electric motors drew more than a megawatt of power — enough to switch on ten thousand light bulbs at once. Airlines are paying close attention, because they are expected to spend about 350 billion dollars on jet fuel in 2026 alone, and fuel prices have climbed sharply this year. A plane that flies for five dollars’ worth of electricity is not just a curiosity; it is a signal that the future of flying may sound very different.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 무게와의 전쟁

Why did it take this long for someone to fly an airplane this big on batteries? The whole story comes down to a single word: weight.

Think of a regular jet. Its engines burn fuel for the entire flight, so the plane grows lighter and lighter as it goes — like a runner who slowly unloads a heavy backpack along the way. An airliner can take off carrying tens of thousands of kilograms of fuel and land far lighter than it started. Batteries work the opposite way: a battery weighs exactly the same whether it is full of charge or completely empty. An electric airplane must carry its heavy backpack for the entire flight and is never allowed to set it down.

The deeper problem is energy density — how much energy fits into each kilogram. One kilogram of jet fuel holds about 12,000 watt-hours of energy. One kilogram of the best modern batteries holds only about 250 watt-hours. Kilogram for kilogram, jet fuel carries nearly fifty times more energy. That is why the X1 needed a mountain of batteries, and why every extra battery makes the plane heavier, which demands more power, which demands still more batteries. Engineers call this cruel loop the “battery spiral.”

Heart Aerospace escaped the spiral by choosing the right battle. The X1 was never meant to cross oceans. It flew for 27 minutes and covered about 125 miles — a short hop. And electric motors have a superpower: they turn almost all of their electricity into motion, reaching about 95 percent efficiency, while jet engines waste most of their fuel’s energy as heat. On short routes between neighboring cities, efficiency matters more than range — and that is exactly where electric planes can win first.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 2031년, 조종석의 미래

The X1 itself will never carry passengers; it is a flying laboratory. The real prize is its successor, the ES-30: a “hybrid” airplane, much like a hybrid car. It would fly the first 200 kilometers on pure battery power, then switch on small generators to stretch its range to about 800 kilometers — enough to hop between neighboring cities. The company says the design could cut an airline’s operating costs by more than 40 percent compared with today’s small turboprop planes. Airlines have already lined up: United Airlines has agreed to buy 100 of them.

Before any of that happens, Heart must build and fly the X2, a second test plane that will prove the hybrid system in the air. If all goes well, the company hopes the ES-30 will begin carrying real passengers by 2031. That is only five years from now — a thirteen-year-old today will be old enough to begin flight training by the time these planes enter service.

Big questions remain. Batteries must get lighter and cheaper. Airports would need enormous chargers — imagine the electricity required to refill a 30-seat airplane in half an hour. Safety rules written for jet engines must be rewritten for electric motors. And tomorrow’s pilots will need to learn to fly machines that land at exactly the same weight they took off, that climb almost silently, and that handle differently from anything in today’s fleets.

Still, the direction is clear. Aviation produces about 2.5 percent of the world’s greenhouse gas emissions, and electric flight is one of the few ways to shrink that number. On August 12, for 27 minutes, an airliner-sized airplane took off, flew, and landed on nothing but batteries. The next time you watch a plane climb into the sky, try a thought experiment: imagine the engines going quiet, the only sound the wind over the wings, and the whole flight costing less than lunch to power. That future is not here yet — but it has already flown once.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are the pilot of the X1 on its first-ever flight. The sun is rising over the runway, you are the only person on board, and no airplane this big has ever flown on batteries before. Write about your flight from the moment the wheels leave the ground to the moment they touch down again — what you see, what you hear (or don’t hear), and what you feel.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include one thing you would want to check or find out during the test flight, explain why that finding matters for the future of electric airplanes, and describe what it would mean if the result turned out different from what you expected.
