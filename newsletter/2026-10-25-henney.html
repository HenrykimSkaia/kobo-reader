상어 피부로 연료를 아끼는 비행기: 세계 첫 '상어피' 에어버스 여객기 취항

2026-10-25 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: Aerospace Global News — World's first 'sharkskin' Airbus A330 enters passenger service

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 플로리다로 간 '상어' 비행기

On October 15, 2026, an Airbus A330-300 lifted off toward Florida with a secret hiding in plain sight. Passengers boarding the Discover Airlines jet saw an ordinary wide-body airplane. They could not see the thin, transparent film stretched over parts of its fuselage and engine nacelles. The airplane, registration D-AIKP, had been given a nickname by its own airline: the “Discover Airlines Shark.” Four days later, on October 19, the airline confirmed the news. This was the first Airbus aircraft ever to enter regular scheduled passenger service wearing a large-scale coat of sharkskin technology.

The film is called AeroSHARK. It was developed by Lufthansa Technik together with Surventis, and it had been fitted to D-AIKP at Dresden Airport in late August. After the fitting came weeks of test flights. Engineers studied the flight data the way a doctor studies test results, checking that the new skin behaved safely and reliably at real airline speeds and altitudes. Only when the data looked good did the airplane return to ordinary passenger duty. The work was part of a wider certification program for the A330ceo family, carried out under the oversight of Europe's aviation safety agency, EASA.

The headline number is small, and that is the point. According to its developers, the film can cut an airplane's fuel use and carbon dioxide emissions by about 1 percent. One percent of a single flight is a rounding error. One percent of a fleet is a different story. Lufthansa Group reported 23 AeroSHARK-equipped aircraft in service as of September 2026, saving more than 20 metric tons of fuel every day between them. A tiny improvement, multiplied by hundreds of flights, becomes tons of fuel that never has to be burned.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 상어 피부가 빠른 이유, 홈의 비밀

To understand the trick, forget the idea that smooth is always fast. A shark looks sleek, but run a finger along its skin the wrong way and it feels like sandpaper. Shark skin is covered in tiny tooth-shaped scales called denticles, each etched with fine grooves. Those grooves are the whole idea. AeroSHARK copies them with a transparent adhesive film carrying microscopic ridges called riblets, each about 50 micrometers tall. Fifty micrometers is thinner than a human hair, and far too small to see from the gate.

Here is why grooves beat smoothness. As an airplane flies, a thin layer of air clings to its skin and gets dragged along. Inside that layer, the air does not flow in neat lines. It tumbles and swirls in tiny whirlpools, and every swirl steals a little energy from the engines. Engineers call this skin-friction drag. The riblets work like lanes painted on a highway. Aligned with the airflow, they guide the tumbling air into orderly channels and keep the wildest swirls from scrubbing directly against the surface. Less scrubbing means less drag, less drag means the engines can push a little less hard, and pushing less hard means burning less fuel.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody built a new airplane, invented a new fuel, or replaced an engine. The same A330 that flew last year is flying now, only wearing a smarter skin. That is why engineers like retrofits. A brand-new airplane design takes a decade and billions of dollars. A film can be stuck onto airplanes that already exist, during ordinary maintenance, and start saving fuel on the very next flight. The airplane did not change. Its relationship with the air did.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 1%의 힘, 그리고 남은 질문

So what happens next? First, watch the numbers grow. If one wide-body jet can prove its 1 percent saving in daily service, airlines will ask the obvious question: why stop at the fuselage and the engine nacelles? Covering more of the airplane, including the wings, could push the saving higher. But every new patch must earn its place. Engineers will check whether the film survives rain, ice, de-icing fluid, and the daily scrub of airport life, and whether a damaged patch can be repaired quickly between flights.

Second, watch the idea spread beyond one airline. Lufthansa Group's 23 equipped aircraft already save more than 20 tons of fuel a day, and every ton of jet fuel not burned is roughly three tons of carbon dioxide not released. That is the kind of arithmetic that turns a curiosity into a fleet decision. The same lesson is hiding in nature's other textbooks. Owls fly almost silently thanks to the fringes on their feathers. Whales swim efficiently with bumpy flippers. Engineers are borrowing all of it, one surface at a time.

Finally, there is a lesson for a future pilot. Flying is often imagined as the romance of the cockpit, but much of it is the discipline of efficiency: choosing the right altitude, the right speed, the right weight. A pilot who understands drag understands why a 1 percent saving is worth chasing. The “Discover Airlines Shark” will not change what passengers see out the window. It changes what the air feels as the airplane passes, and over thousands of flights, what the air feels is what the planet feels too.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are the engineer who fitted the sharkskin film to D-AIKP, and now you must convince one skeptical airline boss to put it on her entire fleet, not just one airplane. She has one question for you, and your whole project depends on your answer. The board meeting starts in ten minutes.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: one thing you would want to check before the meeting (for example, how the film survives a winter of de-icing, or how much fuel your own busiest route would really save) and why that detail matters; how you would explain the 1 percent saving so it sounds big, not small; and what it would mean for the airline if the boss says yes to the whole fleet instead of just one airplane.
