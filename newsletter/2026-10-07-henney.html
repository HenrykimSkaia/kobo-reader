당근으로 셈하는 기린: 덧셈은 되는데 뺄셈은 안 돼?

2026-10-07 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 기린의 당근 수학 실험

Picture this: a giraffe bends its long neck toward a table and studies two yellow boxes. Inside each box are pieces of carrot — a giraffe's favorite treat. A scientist shows the animal how many carrot pieces sit in each box, then closes both lids. Next, the scientist picks up a green box holding a few more carrots and, through a hidden slot, drops them into one of the yellow boxes. The giraffe never sees the final amounts. Now it must choose: which yellow box holds more carrots?

That was the daily quiz for four giraffes at the Barcelona Zoo in Spain. The team behind the test came from the University of Barcelona, led by researcher Iker Loidi. Their goal was simple to state but tricky to prove: can a giraffe do something like mental addition — hold two amounts in its head, combine them, and pick the bigger total?

The results, published in the journal Scientific Reports on June 26, 2026, were a split decision. Two of the giraffes — a female named Nuru and a male named Njano — passed with flying colors, picking the box with more carrots far more often than blind luck would allow. The other two, Nakuru and Yalinga, had shakier report cards. They often chose correctly, but only when a sneaky shortcut was available: they tended to pick whichever box the scientist's hand had touched last. When the researchers removed that clue, their scores fell back to chance.

And there was one more twist. When the scientists reversed the game — taking carrots away instead of adding them — every giraffe flopped. Subtraction, it seems, is a much harder puzzle for a giraffe brain than addition. The story, featured by DOGOnews on October 6, quickly became one of the most talked-about animal studies of the year.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 정말로 '셈'을 한 걸까?

Skeptical readers might ask: did the giraffes really add, or were they just guessing smartly? The researchers worried about exactly that, so they built trapdoors into the experiment. Picking the box a human touched last is not math — it is reading a hint. By sometimes touching both boxes, or making the touching meaningless, the team could tell which giraffes were doing the mental work and which were following shortcuts. Only Nuru and Njano kept winning after the hints were taken away.

There was another trap: size. Carrot pieces are not identical — one chunk can be bigger than another. A giraffe that simply picks the bulkier, heavier-smelling pile might look like it is counting when it is really judging volume. This is why the scientists describe the skill carefully. It is not addition the way you add 7 + 5 on paper, with symbols and carried digits. It is closer to the mental foundation of addition: keeping two amounts in memory and comparing their combined size.

The subtraction failure is just as interesting as the addition success. In test after test, when carrots were removed or shuffled between boxes more than once, every giraffe's accuracy dropped to coin-flip levels. Scientists have seen this exact pattern before — in human toddlers, in pigeons, and in primates. Combining quantities (putting together) seems to be an older, easier mental operation than separating them (taking apart). Your brain, it turns out, may share that bias with a giraffe's.

This was not the team's first surprise with these animals. In a 2023 experiment, the same giraffes had to choose between containers holding different mixes of carrot and zucchini slices — and they reliably picked the mix with proportionally more carrots, a kind of statistical reasoning. Earlier work also showed giraffes outperforming eight other hoofed mammals, including llamas, bison, and rhinos, at telling larger quantities from smaller ones. The number sense was already there; the new study asked how far it goes.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 기린의 수학은 어디에 쓰일까

Why would a giraffe need anything like arithmetic in the first place? The researchers have a guess: life on the savanna is full of quantity decisions. Which acacia tree has more leaves worth the long walk? Is that rival group bigger than mine — should I stand my ground or move on? A brain that can track "more versus less," even roughly, could be the difference between a good meal and a wasted trip. If the skill helps in the wild, evolution would have kept it.

Plenty of questions remain open. Why did Nuru and Njano succeed while their two companions leaned on shortcuts? Is it a natural difference in ability — the giraffe version of some kids being better at math — or did past training at the zoo give the winners an edge? The researchers do not know yet. Another puzzle: does the subtraction failure mean giraffes truly cannot handle taking away, or did the test simply ask too much at once? Future experiments might start with easier take-away tasks and build up slowly.

Outside scientists see the bigger picture. A zoologist at the University of Ferrara in Italy, who was not part of the study, notes that a single experiment is not a revolution — but each one fills in the map of how thinking evolved across the animal kingdom. Complex cognition, the evidence suggests, is not the private property of humans and other primates. It may have evolved independently, more than once, in very different kinds of brains — a pattern biologists call convergent evolution.

For now, the scorecard reads: giraffes 1, subtraction 0. But the real winner is the question itself. Every time scientists design a fairer test, animals surprise them — and every surprise redraws the boundary of what "thinking" means. The next time you see a giraffe calmly chewing leaves, remember: behind those calm eyes, someone might be keeping count.
