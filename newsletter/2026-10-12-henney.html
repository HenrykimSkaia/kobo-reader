Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 스웨덴에서 온 일주일의 전화

Every October, a few phone calls from Sweden and Norway turn working scientists into Nobel laureates. This year's science prizes, announced October 5–7, honored three discoveries: a light switch for the brain, a telescope built from Antarctic ice, and the solution to a century-old mystery of molecular handedness.

Monday's prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel for inventing optogenetics — switching brain cells on and off with light. The idea began in pond water: a protein in the eye spot of a tiny green alga swings open like a gate when light strikes it. Deisseroth put the gene for that protein into the nerve cells of living mice and threaded hair-thin fibers into their brains; blue light through the fibers made the cells fire on command.

Tuesday's Physics prize went to Francis Halzen, who in 1988 proposed an absurd-sounding idea: sink thousands of light sensors into South Pole ice and wait for messengers from deep space. The messengers are neutrinos, nearly massless particles so shy that billions stream through your body every second without a trace. His observatory, IceCube, was finished in 2011. When a rare neutrino strikes an atomic nucleus in the ice, it emits a faint blue flash the sensors record — and IceCube has traced such flashes back to distant galaxies, a brand-new way to observe the universe.

Wednesday's Chemistry prize went to Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai. Hold up your hands: mirror images, yet no twist makes one overlap the other. Many molecules come in left- and right-handed pairs, yet life uses only one hand: your proteins' amino acids are left-handed, your DNA's sugars right-handed. But chemists making such molecules in a flask always got a fifty-fifty mix of both hands. Kagan and Soai found reactions that magnify a tiny initial imbalance until one hand takes over — a possible answer to how life's one-handed chemistry began.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 세 발견이 대단한 이유

Why is a brain light switch revolutionary? In 1979, Francis Crick called controlling one brain-cell type while sparing its neighbors the great dream of neuroscience — probably impossible with the tools of the day. Optogenetics made it real: the algal protein, channelrhodopsin, is a door only light opens. Give a chosen set of neurons the gene for that door, and only those neurons fire when blue light comes on. Today labs worldwide use it to map circuits behind memory, fear, hunger, and sleep — and are testing whether it could restore vision in some blindness by making the eye's remaining cells light-sensitive.

IceCube is a different marvel: patience beating impossibility. A neutrino almost never touches anything, so catching one means watching an enormous target — a billion tons of Antarctic ice, watched by five thousand frozen-in sensors — and waiting. Sketched in 1988, the observatory took 23 years to build. In 2013 IceCube caught the first high-energy neutrinos from far outside our galaxy, later matching some to flaring galaxies billions of light-years away — letting humanity study the universe through particles born in its most violent events.

The chemistry prize reaches back to a puzzle Louis Pasteur noticed in 1848: why does life insist on one hand? In 1986 Kagan showed a catalyst from slightly impure ingredients could still yield a strongly one-handed product — the reaction amplified the imbalance itself, like a rumor growing louder each retelling. Soai went further, designing a reaction whose product helps make more of itself, so a microscopic excess of one hand snowballs into dominance. It matters twice: practically, it transformed drug manufacturing, since a molecule's two mirror versions can act very differently in the body; deeply, it hints at how non-living chemistry chose a side and became life.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 노벨상이 가리키는 미래

Prizes look backward, but each discovery points forward — and the next chapters are unwritten.

Optogenetics remains mostly a lab tool; using it in people would mean delivering genes into the brain and implanting light sources — serious safety and ethical questions. The nearer horizon is the eye, where researchers are exploring treatments that could return partial sight in inherited blindness. Farther out: if scientists map exactly which circuits produce a memory or emotion, medicine could repair them with precision today's drugs cannot match — raising the question of where healing ends and redesigning begins.

Neutrino astronomy is just beginning: IceCube-Gen2 would be roughly ten times larger, aiming to pinpoint the cosmic engines — colliding black holes, exploding stars — firing these particles across the universe. Together with light and gravitational waves, neutrinos complete a trio of cosmic messengers — and each new messenger has historically rewritten the textbooks.

The handedness mystery keeps its biggest question: how did the first imbalance appear? Some suspect the answer fell from the sky — certain meteorites carry excess one-handed amino acids, hinting space nudged life's choice. Labs now replay life's origin under early-Earth conditions to see if a one-handed world emerges.

A pattern hides in all three prizes: Halzen waited 38 years from idea to Nobel; Kagan's key experiment is 40 years old; the algal protein sat in pond scum for eons before anyone asked what it could do. The committee rewarded the same thing three times — curiosity that refused to hurry. That is the part a thirteen-year-old can start today: the questions you follow for decades are the ones most likely to change the world.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are a young scientist who just discovered a strange light-sensitive protein in ordinary pond water. You have a wild hunch: one day, this protein could let doctors switch brain cells on and off with light. Right now, though, all you have is a jar of green water — and a skeptical lab director who controls the funding. Write the two-minute pitch you would deliver to convince the director to bet on your idea.

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: what your discovery is and the single first experiment you would run to test it, why controlling brain cells with light could matter for medicine, and what it would mean if your experiment produced the exact opposite of what you expected.
