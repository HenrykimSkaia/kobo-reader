달 뒷면에서 본 다섯 개의 섬광: Artemis II 과학 보고서 대공개

2026-10-18 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: NASA Science — Artemis II Preliminary Lunar Science Report

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 달 뒷면, 1시간의 개기일식, 그리고 다섯 개의 섬광

Imagine flying around the Moon — and watching space rocks crash into it. In real time. With your own eyes.

That is what happened to the four astronauts of NASA’s Artemis II mission, the first crewed journey to the Moon’s neighborhood in more than fifty years. On October 7, NASA published the mission’s Preliminary Lunar Science Report — photographs, spoken observations, drawings, and hand-written annotations the crew made along the way. The full report is now open to scientists and the public.

The most dramatic chapter is titled, simply, “Impact!”

Lunar researchers had asked the crew to do something unusual: watch the Moon in the dark and look for tiny flashes of light. The chance came during a planned radio silence on the far side of the Moon — the half that never faces Earth. From the crew’s viewpoint, a total solar eclipse was under way, blotting out the Sun for nearly an hour. In that darkness, they saw five extremely faint “pinprick” bursts of light on the surface, from about 46,000 miles away.

The crew’s voices tell the story best. “Dude! No way!” one astronaut exclaimed. Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen and Reid Wiseman saw one flash at the same instant — “It’s just like a ‘bloop!’” Hansen said. Scientists in Houston later ruled out cosmic rays and reflections from the spacecraft, and confirmed the sightings were real impacts.

The rocks were tiny — each only a couple of inches across, about the size of a golf ball. But they were traveling thousands of miles per hour. NASA says these may be the faintest impact flashes ever seen with the unaided eye. Apollo astronauts saw similar flashes, but this is the first time in over half a century that humans have watched the Moon being struck.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 빛나는 플라즈마 구름과 ‘차분한 무지개’

Why does a tiny rock make a visible flash? The answer starts with something the Moon doesn’t have: air.

On Earth, the atmosphere is a shield. Small space rocks burn up high above us, painting the streaks we call shooting stars. The Moon has no such shield, so projectiles slam freely into the surface, and all their motion energy turns to heat in an instant. Rock and surface vaporize together, creating a brief cloud of glowing plasma — hot enough to shine like a tiny starburst, even from 46,000 miles away. The eclipse’s darkness was essential: it was like turning off every light in a theater so you can see a single firefly.

But these flashes aren’t just a show. They give scientists a rare dataset for a serious question: how often do small impacts happen? The answer is essential for assessing long-term risks to future lunar habitats. A Moon base must survive for decades, and knowing the rain of micrometeorites helps engineers design walls, windows, and shelters that can take the hits.

The report also reveals a side of the Moon most people never imagine: its colors. The crew described a “muted rainbow” — soft blues, browns, and greens across the gray surface. The blues point to volcanic rocks rich in titanium. The browns reflect different levels of iron. The greens signal glass formed by ancient eruptions or asteroid impacts. Astronaut Christina Koch called one greenish area “a jaundiced cheek of the Moon.” Future explorers could use these colors to spot resources — a vital skill near the lunar south pole, where extreme lighting makes shadows hard to read.

Then there is the terminator — the moving line between lunar day and night. Along it, low-angle sunlight throws craters and mountains into sharp relief. Pilot Victor Glover called it “the most striking thing that I’ve seen so far,” and Koch gasped, “you can actually see mountains.”

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 릴레이 경주 — 달 과학은 계속된다

The eclipse gave the crew one more gift: a laboratory in the sky.

With the Sun blotted out for nearly an hour, Mars and Saturn, distant galaxies, and zodiacal light appeared — sunlight reflecting off dust between the planets. Best of all, the crew could study the Sun’s corona directly. The corona is one of the Sun’s great mysteries: millions of degrees hotter than the surface, for reasons still not understood. Its plasma drives space weather — invisible storms that can damage spacecraft electronics and endanger astronauts.

Looking homeward, the crew captured something no satellite ever could: the entire night side of Earth at once, lit only by moonlight. Scientists found auroras at both poles, lightning over the Congo basin, city lights across northern Africa and the Iberian peninsula, a huge Saharan dust plume — and Venus, capping the zodiacal light. “The Earth is smiling at us!” the crew cheered.

So what comes next? NASA calls science “a relay race.” The Artemis II crew built on decades of earlier data — and their observations now become the next runners’ foundation. The mission also tested how astronauts and ground teams work together, from new flight-controller roles to fresh training methods. Those lessons will shape Artemis III, which will land astronauts on the Moon.

The biggest open question may be the simplest. Somewhere on the Moon, unseen by any telescope, smaller flashes are happening right now. The next crew — perhaps including a future pilot reading this — will be watching.
