우주정거장에 간 피카츄: 67일간의 궤도 여행

2026-10-04 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 67일 동안 지구를 1,040바퀴 돈 인형

On April 11, 2026, a cargo spacecraft called Cygnus lifted off from Earth carrying food, water, science equipment — and one very unusual passenger: a plush Pikachu. For the next 67 days, the yellow plush floated through the corridors of the International Space Station, watching Earth spin by through the windows. On June 17, it climbed back aboard a SpaceX Dragon cargo capsule and returned home — after circling our planet about 1,040 times.

The European Space Agency (ESA) told this story to the world on September 19, 2026, and it was not just a cute anecdote. The plush’s trip was timed to kick off World Space Week, which runs from October 4 to 10 this year — starting today. To celebrate, ESA has built a special exhibition that mixes real space science with Pokémon characters. Visitors can explore themed displays about Europe’s space missions, find out what life is really like aboard the space station, and learn how ESA studies the Sun, the Solar System, and the wider universe.

The exhibition tour began at ESA’s headquarters in Paris in late September and will travel to museums and other venues across Europe through April 2027. The fun extends into the digital world as well: from October 4, 2026 through April 30, 2027, players visiting participating science and space museums across Europe can join special Pokémon GO activities, including One-Star Raids featuring an “Astronaut Pikachu” and space-themed research tasks.

“Inspiring future generations is a key part of ESA’s mission,” said Josef Aschbacher, ESA’s Director General. In other words, a plush toy can be a teaching tool — if it makes a teenager look up and wonder what it is like to work among the stars.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 인형이 우주에서 하는 진짜 일

Why would a serious space agency bother sending a stuffed toy into orbit? The answer goes back to the earliest days of spaceflight. Russian cosmonauts have a beloved tradition: before launch, they hang a small plush toy — a “zero-gravity indicator” — above their cockpit. The moment it starts to float, they know, without reading a single instrument, that they have reached weightlessness. When SpaceX flew its first crew in 2020, the astronauts brought a sparkling toy dinosaur named Tremor. Toys are the simplest, oldest weightless detectors in space.

There is real science hiding behind the cuteness. Floating in the station’s Cupola — the seven-windowed observation deck built by ESA, where the famous photo of Pikachu drifting was taken — a plush toy shows how microgravity treats everyday objects: no up, no down, everything drifting until it bumps into something. During its 67 days, the Pikachu circled Earth once every 90 minutes — about sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every single day. That is how the total reached 1,040 orbits.

ESA has reasons beyond inspiration. Life aboard the station is full of experiments that matter down on Earth: how crystals grow without gravity, how plants respond to space radiation, how the human body changes during long missions. Europe’s contributions include the Columbus laboratory, the Solar Orbiter probe watching the Sun, and the JUICE mission now cruising toward Jupiter. By pairing these serious missions with Pokémon, ESA is doing something clever: meeting young people where their curiosity already lives. A teenager who comes for Pikachu might stay to learn about the solar storms that engineers must track — the same storms that can threaten satellites, power grids, and one day, the pilots and astronauts flying through them.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 오늘 시작되는 우주 축제

World Space Week — October 4 to 10 — is the largest space celebration on Earth, and it exists because of a single date: October 4, 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite. From that beach-ball-sized beep in the sky to a plush Pokémon floating in the Cupola, the journey took just 69 years.

The ESA–Pokémon exhibition is only the beginning. As it travels across Europe through April 2027, it will reach cities where most teenagers will never meet an astronaut — and it only has to spark one career. The space industry needs pilots, engineers, doctors, mechanics, and programmers; every astronaut was once a teenager who looked up. The path from the cockpit to the stars is shorter than it looks: many astronauts started as military or test pilots, because the skills are cousins — reading instruments under pressure, trusting checklists, staying calm when something unexpected happens.

And the open questions are the best part. What would you send to space if you could pack one small item? Scientists choose experiments, but plush passengers have flown for decades because wonder is a kind of fuel. The Pikachu’s journey also asks a sharper question: if a toy can go to orbit and come back, what is stopping you? The first person to walk on Mars is probably alive right now — possibly sitting in a classroom, possibly reading this.

For now, try a thought experiment: imagine your own childhood toy floating beside the Cupola’s windows, Earth filling the glass below — blue oceans, white cloud swirls, city lights flickering on at the night edge. You reach out, and it drifts just past your fingertips, the way things move in a dream where you can fly. That feeling — weightless, curious, a little bit brave — is exactly what space agencies are trying to share. Sometimes the shortest path to a rocket is a plush toy.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are the first teenager chosen to bring a childhood toy to the Moon. You may pack one small item in your spacesuit pocket. Write about your choice: what it is, why it matters to you, and what happens when you let go of it in lunar gravity — one-sixth of Earth’s. Does it fall like a stone, or float like a balloon? What does it look like against the black sky?

Write 150–200 words in English. Include one thing you would want to find out or observe during your stay, explain why that finding would matter for the future of pilots and astronauts, and describe what it would mean if the result turned out different from what you expected.
