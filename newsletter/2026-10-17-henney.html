음속의 벽을 다시 넘다: 초음속 여객기 Overture의 귀환

2026-10-17 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: N24

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 초음속 여객기를 찍어내는 공장

Imagine this: you finish breakfast in New York, board a plane, and land in London before lunch is even served. For more than twenty years, that kind of speed existed only in old photographs. This week, it moved one big step closer to reality.

In Greensboro, North Carolina, a company called Boom Supersonic has just finished building a factory with an ambitious name: the Overture Superfactory. It stands beside the Piedmont Triad International Airport, and it is the first factory in the United States built specifically to manufacture supersonic passenger airplanes. “Supersonic” means faster than sound — and the airplane it will build, called Overture, is designed to fly at Mach 1.7, roughly twice as fast as the jets flying today.

The numbers are striking. Overture is planned to carry 64 to 80 passengers in an all-premium cabin, fly up to 4,250 nautical miles, and run on up to 100 percent sustainable aviation fuel — fuel made from plants and waste instead of crude oil. The new factory is expected to produce up to 33 Overtures a year, with a second assembly line planned to double that to 66. More than 130 aircraft have already been ordered or reserved by United Airlines, American Airlines, and Japan Airlines, a backlog worth about 26 billion dollars.

North Carolina’s governor joined the announcement, and economists project the program could bring more than 2,400 jobs and 32.3 billion dollars to the state’s economy over twenty years. The building itself is designed to be LEED certified, using at least 40 percent less energy than a typical factory. None of this guarantees the plane will fly. But twenty-three years after the last supersonic airliner retired, someone has finally built the factory that could bring it back.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 음속의 벽과 소닉붐

To understand why this matters, you first need to understand the sound barrier — and it is not actually a wall.

When a plane flies, it pushes air out of the way, creating pressure waves that spread outward at the speed of sound. Think of a speedboat: as it moves through water, it leaves a V-shaped wake behind it. Sound works the same way. When a plane flies slower than sound, the waves spread out ahead of it like ripples. But when the plane catches up with its own sound waves and passes them, all those waves pile up into one sharp wall of pressure — the sonic boom. On the ground, it sounds like a thunderclap, sometimes two in a row.

This boom is the reason supersonic passenger flight disappeared. The Concorde, the most famous supersonic airliner, cruised at Mach 2.04 — more than twice the speed of sound — at 60,000 feet, so high that passengers could see the curve of the Earth. But its thunderous boom meant governments banned it from flying supersonic over land; it could only go fast over oceans. Between the noise rules, thirsty engines, and expensive tickets, the Concorde retired in 2003.

Boom’s bet is that engineering can solve what rules once killed. Its small demonstrator jet, the XB-1, broke the sound barrier over California’s Mojave Desert in January 2025, proving the aerodynamics work. The full-size Overture uses a long, carefully shaped nose and fuselage designed to spread out the pressure waves so the boom fades before it reaches the ground — a technique called “boomless cruise.” Its new Symphony engines are also designed to cruise at supersonic speed without afterburners, the fuel-guzzling “reheat” system that made the Concorde so loud and expensive. And because the engines can burn sustainable aviation fuel, each flight could produce far less carbon than one burning ordinary kerosene.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 콩코드의 빈자리를 채울 수 있을까

So will you one day fly faster than sound? The honest answer is: maybe — but several hard problems stand between the factory and your seat.

The first is certification. Before Overture can carry a single passenger, it must prove to aviation authorities that it is as safe as any normal airliner — a process that takes years of test flights. The XB-1 proved the shape works, but the full-size engines still need ground testing, and the first Overture flight is targeted for around 2028, with passenger service aimed at the end of the decade. History suggests these dates often slip; being skeptical of timelines is a healthy habit.

The second is noise — not in the cabin, but on the ground. The United States has banned civil supersonic flight over land since 1973. A new rule is now being written that would replace the blanket ban with a noise standard: fly as fast as you like, as long as your boom is quiet enough. Overture was designed to pass exactly that test. Whether it actually does will be decided by measurements, not marketing.

The third is the planet. Even on sustainable fuel, a supersonic jet burns more energy per passenger than a normal airliner. Sustainable aviation fuel can cut carbon sharply, but the world does not yet make enough of it. Tickets will also be premium — think business class, not budget fares. The question is not just “can we fly this fast?” but “should we, and for whom?”

For a future pilot, though, the story is pure fuel for the imagination. Supersonic flight needs a new generation of pilots trained on new simulators, flying routes no civilian pilot has flown in twenty years. The Concorde retired before you were born. The pilots who will fly its successor are teenagers today — quite possibly reading a newsletter like this one.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are 26 years old and have just earned your pilot’s license. Boom Supersonic invites you to apply for its very first class of Overture pilots — but the selection panel asks one hard question: “A supersonic ticket will cost as much as business class, and each passenger uses more fuel than on a normal flight. Why should this airplane exist at all?”

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: your strongest reason why a supersonic airliner should exist; how you would answer the fuel and cost concern; and if the panel disagreed with you, what that disagreement would teach you about the future of flight.
