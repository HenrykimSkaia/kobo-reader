달에 갑자기 나타난 거대 분화구의 정체

2026-10-01 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: TIME for Kids

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 달 지도 위 수상한 얼룩

On October 24, 2025, Robert Wagner was doing the most boring part of his job: a routine data-quality check. Wagner is an image-processing specialist at Intuitive Machines who works with pictures from NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, a spacecraft that has been circling the Moon since 2009. He was scanning a giant map of the Moon — hundreds of images stitched together — when something made him freeze. A large, unusually bright spot, circled by a dark ring, stared back at him from the Moon’s eastern edge. “I just stopped, dropped everything, and started looking into what that spot was,” he said later. “It was by far the most obvious impact debris pattern I’ve ever seen in one of these images.”

What Wagner had found was a brand-new crater — one missing from pictures taken just a year and a half earlier. It measured 728 feet across, about the length of two football fields, and 141 feet deep: enough room to stack three yellow school buses. Scientists named it McGetchin, after Tom McGetchin, a pioneering lunar geologist who helped train the Apollo astronauts. The before-and-after images showed it formed sometime between April 11 and May 22, 2024, when a comet or asteroid roughly the size of a three-to-six-story building slammed into the lunar surface at tens of thousands of kilometers per hour.

On September 16, 2026, two papers in the journal Science Advances announced the discovery. The verdict: McGetchin is the largest newly formed crater ever found anywhere in the solar system, and an impact this big strikes the Moon only about once a century — some models say once every 132 years. “This event constitutes a statistically rare, effectively once-in-a-lifetime observation,” the authors wrote. Nobody on Earth saw the flash when it happened. The Moon kept its secret for a year and a half — until one person looked closely enough to notice.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 분화구가 말해주는 것

How do you spot something new on a world that never changes? You watch it change. The Orbiter’s Wide Angle Camera photographs the entire Moon every month, and has since 2010. Software stacks hundreds of these pictures; anything unchanged shows up gray, anything changed glows bright or dark. That is how Wagner’s bright spot appeared — fresh soil thrown outward by a violent impact, ringed by a dark halo where the surface had been shaken. Stripes in the image come from slight differences in sunlight between old and new photos, like comparing pictures taken at different times of day.

The Moon is a perfect target for space rocks because it has almost no atmosphere. On Earth, thick air burns up most incoming meteors — the shooting stars you wish on are rocks dying in the sky. The Moon has no such shield, so every rock arrives at full speed. Small ones hit constantly: craters about 30 feet wide form roughly 140 times a year. But a building-sized rock is another story. Before McGetchin, the biggest freshly made crater ever found was only about 230 feet wide — less than a third of McGetchin’s width. The models said an impact this size should happen roughly once per century. Here it was.

A fresh crater is a gift: a controlled experiment performed by nature. The second Science Advances paper studied what McGetchin did to the ground around it — how the blast changed the temperature of the lunar soil, called regolith, and how far the debris spread. Why does that matter? Scientists date planetary surfaces by counting craters: more craters means older ground. But that clock only works if we understand exactly how impacts reshape the surface — and every new crater is a chance to check the math. There is a practical reason too. With NASA’s Artemis program preparing to return astronauts to the Moon, this time to stay, engineers need to know that the ground under a future moon base is still being remodeled by the sky.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 아르테미스 시대의 달

McGetchin is not just a record-breaker; it previews the Moon’s future as a workplace. NASA plans a sustained human presence there, and private companies are joining in — Intuitive Machines, Wagner’s own employer, already lands robotic spacecraft on the surface. A moon base cannot be designed as if the ground were frozen in time. Future lunar pilots and astronauts will need constantly updated maps, the way airline pilots get fresh weather charts before every flight. The LRO’s long watch — 17 years, more than a thousand newly spotted craters — shows what patient observation can do.

Plenty of questions remain. Was the impactor a rocky asteroid or an icy comet? The answer is hidden in the debris, and future orbiters or landers might read it like a fingerprint. Why did this impact disturb such an unusually large patch of ground? And are the “once a century” models actually right — or has the Moon been hit by more big rocks than we thought, with the evidence hiding in plain sight? Follow-up studies will track how McGetchin’s fresh soil settles and cools over the coming years.

There is a quieter lesson here, one that has nothing to do with rockets. Wagner did not find McGetchin with a powerful new telescope or a lucky guess. He found it because he looked — really looked — at a routine map on an ordinary workday, and refused to scroll past something strange. The Moon is often called a dead world, a museum of ancient impacts. McGetchin proves it is alive in slow motion, still being sculpted while we watch. For anyone who dreams of flying — whether through Earth’s skies or beyond them — that is the real message: the universe rewards the people who pay attention.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are on the LRO image-analysis team, and tomorrow’s monthly Moon map shows a new bright spot with a dark ring — just like McGetchin. You are the first person to see it. How would you prove it is a fresh crater and not a trick of the light?

Write 150–200 words in English. Describe one check you would run (for example, comparing images, checking the date, or measuring the spot) and explain why that check matters. Then add your own idea: if your check gives a result you did not expect, what could that unexpected result teach you?
