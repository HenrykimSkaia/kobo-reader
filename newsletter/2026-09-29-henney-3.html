솜사탕보다 가벼운 거대 행성 발견

2026-09-29 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: DOGOnews

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 솜사탕 행성의 발견

Imagine a planet as big as Jupiter but as light as cotton candy. Astronomers have just found two of them.

They are called TOI-791 b and TOI-791 c. Each is about the size of Jupiter, yet incredibly light — less dense than cotton candy. They orbit a star much like our Sun, 1,113 light-years away. The light we see from that star today left it over a thousand years ago.

Astronomers call such worlds "super-puffs," and only a handful are known. Finding two in one system is even rarer. "Their extremely low densities make them fascinating targets for understanding how planetary systems form and evolve," said Dr. George Dransfield, who led the study. These fluffy giants could help answer a huge question: how are planets born?

The discovery began with volunteers — ordinary people studying data from NASA's TESS telescope, which watches stars for tiny dips in brightness. They noticed this star's light dipping again and again in a regular pattern: planets passing in front of it.

To confirm, Dr. Dransfield's team turned to telescopes worldwide, especially ASTEP in Antarctica. In the polar winter the sun never rises, so the team could watch entire transits — each lasting over 11 hours, the longest ever seen from the ground.

Then they measured the planets: sizes from how much starlight was blocked, masses from how the two planets' gravity tugged on each other. Size plus mass gave density — and the answer was shocking.

One more surprise: the planets orbit in a perfect rhythm — five orbits of the inner one for every three of the outer. This "resonance" suggests they were born together from the same disk of gas and dust. The findings appeared in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society on June 25, 2026.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 안 보이는 행성을 찾는 법

How do you find a planet you cannot see? At 1,113 light-years away, TOI-791 b and c are far too dim to photograph. Astronomers use a clever trick instead: the transit method.

When a planet passes between its star and us, it blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light — like a fly crossing a distant street lamp. By measuring these dips, astronomers can tell a planet is there, how big it is, and how long its year lasts.

NASA's TESS telescope is a champion at this, staring at thousands of stars waiting for dips. But it produces more data than scientists can check alone. Citizen scientists — students, teachers, space fans — spotted the repeating dips around this star. Without them, these planets might still be unknown.

A dip is only a clue. To confirm a planet, astronomers must watch complete transits from the ground. The problem: each transit here lasts over 11 hours, and at most observatories sunrise ruins the measurement midway. Antarctica solved it — during polar winter the sun stays down for months, giving ASTEP unbroken darkness to record full transits, the longest ever from Earth's surface.

From each transit the team learned the planet's size: the deeper the dip, the bigger the planet — a basketball blocks more lamplight than a marble. Then the harder part: the weight. The two planets tug on each other with gravity, making their orbits run slightly early or late. From those tiny timing shifts the team calculated the masses — like guessing two dancers' weights by watching how they pull each other as they spin.

Finally, density: mass divided by volume. The result broke expectations. Density tells astronomers what a planet is made of without touching it: high means rock and metal like Earth, low means gas like Jupiter — but impossibly low means something entirely new, a world our theories never predicted.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 풀리지 않은 수수께끼

Now the biggest question: how can planets this big be this light? Nothing in our solar system looks like this. Scientists love a rule-breaker — it means something new to learn.

The leading theory sounds like science fiction: each super-puff has a small solid core — perhaps only a few times Earth's mass — wrapped in an enormous blanket of hydrogen and helium. The thick atmosphere puffs it to Jupiter size while the total weight stays amazingly low. Picture a giant balloon with a marble at its center.

If true, it changes how we think planets grow. The classic story says a core must reach about ten times Earth's mass before it can pull in thick gas. Super-puffs seem to have grabbed far more gas than their tiny cores should hold. Did they form farther out, where gas was plentiful, then drift inward? Nobody knows yet.

So the team is turning to the James Webb Space Telescope. Webb can split starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere and read which gases it holds — like tasting alien air from a thousand light-years away. Each gas leaves a unique fingerprint of colors. Thick hydrogen and helium with little else would confirm the balloon theory. Something unexpected — water vapor or strange clouds — sends scientists back to the drawing board, which is often when the most exciting discoveries happen.

The stakes go beyond two odd planets. Our solar system, with its rocky Earths and heavy Jupiters, is just one way to build planets. The universe is more creative than we imagined.

And remember: this story started with volunteers spotting tiny dips on a screen. The next great discovery might be hiding in data anyone can examine. Maybe even you.
