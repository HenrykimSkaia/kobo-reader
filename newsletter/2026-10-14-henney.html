조종사 없이 착륙하는 비행기: PC-12 PRO의 세이프티 오토랜드

2026-10-14 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: Pilatus Aircraft

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 조종사가 조종하지 않아도 착륙하는 비행기

Imagine you are a passenger in a small airplane when the pilot suddenly cannot fly. There is no co-pilot. The controls sit untouched, and nobody else on board has ever flown a plane. In that moment, a single button on the panel can save everyone: it tells the airplane to land itself.

That is the idea behind Safety Autoland, the emergency system now certified on the brand-new Pilatus PC-12 PRO. The Swiss manufacturer Pilatus announced that both the American Federal Aviation Administration and Europe's EASA have certified the system for this airplane. During the final certification flights, the system flew entire landings completely on its own — from the moment it was switched on to touchdown, radio calls, and even shutting down the engine.

The PC-12 PRO is a single-engine turboprop airplane that can carry around ten passengers. It is one of the most popular small airplanes in the world, and it is very often flown by a single pilot — a business owner, a doctor, or a family flying themselves. That is exactly why this system matters. A large airliner carries two pilots, so if one becomes ill, the other takes over. In a single-pilot airplane, there is no backup human. Safety Autoland is designed to be that backup.

When the system activates — either because someone on board presses the emergency button or because the system notices that the pilot has not touched the controls for a long time — it takes over the airplane completely. First, it announces to everyone on board that the automatic landing mode has begun. Then it starts a rapid chain of decisions: it reads the weather, checks the fuel, scans the terrain below, and reviews every airport within reach. From that information, it picks the safest place to land — a long enough runway, favorable wind, acceptable weather — and flies the airplane there by itself.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 버튼 하나 뒤에 숨은 두뇌

Safety Autoland is sometimes called "emergency autoland," and it was developed by the American avionics company Garmin. The PC-12 PRO is not the first airplane to carry it. The technology is already certified on the Cirrus Vision Jet, Piper's M600 and M700, Daher's TBM 940 and 960, and several other small aircraft. But each new certification matters, because it proves the system again, on a new airplane, under real conditions.

To understand how remarkable this is, it helps to know what landing normally requires. A pilot approaching an airport must manage speed, descent angle, the flaps that give the wing extra lift at low speed, and the landing gear. They must watch the wind, because a strong crosswind can push a small airplane sideways. They must talk to air traffic control, follow instructions, and stay ready for a go-around — climbing away to try again — if something looks wrong. Landing is one of the busiest moments of flying, and it happens while the ground rushes toward the airplane at more than 150 kilometers per hour.

Safety Autoland handles all of that. It extends the flaps and lowers the landing gear on its own schedule. It turns on the lights and, if icing is a danger, the de-icing systems. It steers a route that avoids mountains and tall towers. It even speaks on the radio, broadcasting the emergency on the right frequencies so that controllers and other pilots know what is happening. When the wheels touch down, it brakes, steers down the runway, brings the airplane to a stop, and shuts down the engine. It can also alert emergency services, so help is waiting on the ground.

Think of it as an extremely calm, extremely focused robot co-pilot that never panics and never gets tired. It does not replace the pilot on normal flights. Pilots still fly, train, and make every decision. The system is more like a lifeboat: invisible on good days, and suddenly the most important thing on board on a bad one. Pilatus says the PC-12 already has an outstanding safety record; Safety Autoland adds one more layer of protection for the rarest and most dangerous emergency — a pilot who cannot fly.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 조종사의 일은 어떻게 바뀔까

Safety Autoland raises a fascinating question: if an airplane can land itself in an emergency, how much of flying can a computer handle? The honest answer is: a lot — and also less than you might think.

Modern airliners already fly most of a trip on autopilot. Computers hold the altitude, follow the route, and can even perform automatic landings in fog so thick that a human could not see the runway. What computers cannot do is make judgment calls in situations nobody programmed for: birds in both engines, a passenger collapsing, a runway suddenly closed. The great stories of aviation heroism — like the 2009 landing on the Hudson River in New York, when a captain glided a powerless airliner onto the water and every person on board survived — are stories of human judgment, not of computers.

So the future of the cockpit is probably not "pilots disappear." It is more like "pilots become managers of flying." Technology handles the routine work and the rare emergency; the human handles everything that is new, strange, or uncertain. That is what makes systems like Safety Autoland so interesting: they do not replace the pilot, they extend what a pilot — or even a passenger — can survive.

There are still open questions. How does it feel to press the button, knowing the airplane is now on its own? How do engineers test a system that must work perfectly the very first time it is ever truly needed? And who is responsible if the automated landing makes a mistake? Engineers, regulators, and pilots are debating these questions right now. For someone who dreams of becoming a pilot, this is a front-row view of the future: the cockpit you fly in will have a smart machine beside you. Learning to work with it — trusting it, checking it, and taking over when it cannot — may be one of the most important skills of the next generation of pilots.
