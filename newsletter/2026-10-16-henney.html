조종사 없이 5,000km를 날다: Joby 자율비행기의 대륙 횡단 투어

2026-10-16 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Source: EIN Presswire

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 조종석이 비어 있는 비행기의 대륙 횡단

Picture this. A small airplane lines up on the runway, its engine humming. It speeds forward, lifts off, climbs, turns, and flies on for hours. Then it descends toward a faraway airport, lines up, and lands — smoothly, on its own. There is a person sitting in the cockpit, but their hands never touch the controls. This is not science fiction. This is not science fiction. On October 2, an aircraft like this touched down at Portland International Airport in Oregon, and its visit is making aviation history.

The visitor was the J208, an autonomous test aircraft built by a California company called Joby Aviation. The Portland stop was part of the company's 2026 Electric Skies Tour: a demonstration flight across the entire United States and back, covering 6,665 miles. On the eastbound leg, the J208 flew 3,199 miles from Concord, California to the Outer Banks of North Carolina. During that whole stretch, the safety pilot sitting onboard made zero control inputs. The plane taxied, took off, cruised, and landed entirely by itself.

“By itself” needs one important correction: a human was still in charge. A remote pilot on the ground served as the pilot in command, actively supervising the flight from as far as 2,323 miles away — updating the flight path and handling radio calls with air traffic controllers. The aircraft is a modified Cessna Caravan fitted with Joby's autonomous flight system. Across its test program and the national tour, the J208 has completed more than 400 flights and 800 automated flight hours, and has taken part in three U.S. military exercises.

Why fly a plane with no hands on the controls? Joby and its partners in Oregon say the answer is practical: moving medical supplies, urgent cargo, and disaster-relief goods. In a state where a snowstorm can cut a town off from its roads, an aircraft that can fly a supply route on its own could save lives. The Oregon Department of Aviation, which manages 28 state airports, hosted the demonstration to study exactly these uses.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 자율비행은 어떻게 작동하는가

To understand autonomy, start with something you already know: the autopilot. A regular autopilot is like cruise control in a car: it holds the speed and follows the road, but a driver must stay alert and take over at any moment. Full autonomy is different: it is like a self-driving car that parks itself in a crowded lot. The J208 does not just hold a course; it carries out the whole chain of decisions, from rolling down the taxiway to touching the runway.

Try a thought experiment. Imagine flying a plane while blindfolded, allowed to ask your co-pilot only yes-or-no questions about what is outside. Now give that co-pilot superpowers: eyes that see in every direction at once, a memory of every map ever made, and the ability to solve a thousand math problems per second. That is roughly what the autonomy software does. Sensors act as the eyes, measuring where the plane is and what surrounds it. Software acts as the brain, comparing sensor reports with the planned route and deciding what to do next. Motors and control surfaces act as the hands, moving the ailerons, rudder, and throttle.

The “supervised” part matters as much as the “autonomous” part. Joby uses what it calls supervised autonomy: the software flies, but a remote pilot watches everything, talks to air traffic control, and can change the plan. Think of a student driver with an instructor who has their own brake pedal — except the instructor sits in a control room hundreds of miles away. During some phases of flight, a safety pilot also rides onboard as backup.

Here is a surprising fact: airplanes may be easier to automate than cars. A car must handle pedestrians, bicycles, and unpredictable drivers at every intersection. An airplane flies in a sky with almost no traffic, along routes planned in advance, in airspace where every aircraft announces its position. The hardest parts — takeoff and landing — are exactly what the J208 has demonstrated it can do alone.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 조종사의 미래는 어떻게 바뀌는가

The big question everyone asks: will pilots disappear? The honest answer is that the job is changing, not vanishing. A remote pilot still needs a pilot's license, a pilot's judgment, and a pilot's calm under pressure — they just sit in a control room instead of a cockpit. History offers a useful hint. When autopilots arrived decades ago, people predicted empty cockpits, yet today there are more pilots than ever. Each wave of automation has moved pilots one rung up the ladder, from steering the plane to managing the whole mission.

The next proving ground is already chosen. Oregon is part of a five-state coalition called uFLY, working with the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration's eVTOL Integration Pilot Program. The program aims to figure out how autonomous and electric aircraft can safely share the sky with regular planes. The J208's tour stopped in Utah, Arizona, Oklahoma, and Oregon on purpose: deserts, mountains, and coastlines each stress the technology differently, and every mile of data helps regulators write the rules of tomorrow's airspace.

Plenty of questions remain open. How do you certify software the way you certify an engine? What happens in a thunderstorm, when sensors struggle and the runway disappears behind rain? Will passengers ever trust a flight with no one up front — and does it matter if the first jobs are cargo, not people? Engineers, regulators, and the public will answer these together over the coming years, one test flight at a time.

For a future pilot, the lesson is exciting, not scary. The cockpit of 2040 may look more like a mission-control desk, and the pilots who thrive will combine stick-and-rudder skill with an understanding of software and systems. The dream of flight is not being replaced — it is being upgraded. Every revolution in aviation, from the Wright brothers to the jet age, began with someone young deciding the sky was worth studying.

Essay 과제 — 에세이를 써보세요.

Imagine you are the remote pilot in command of a J208 carrying emergency medical supplies to a mountain town cut off by a snowstorm. Halfway there, your screen shows a storm cell growing over the only mountain pass on the route. The autonomy software suggests flying around it, which adds 40 minutes, but the hospital says the supplies are needed within the hour. What do you decide, and why?

Write 150–200 words in English. Include: the decision you would make and why it matters most; what information you would check first before deciding; and if the outcome surprised you, what that would teach the engineers.
