솜사탕보다 가벼운 거대 행성 발견

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 솜사탕 행성의 발견

Imagine a planet as big as Jupiter, but as light as cotton candy. It sounds impossible, but astronomers have just found two of them.

The two planets are named TOI-791 b and TOI-791 c. Each one is about the size of Jupiter, the largest planet in our solar system. But they are incredibly light — less dense than cotton candy. They orbit a star much like our Sun, about 1,113 light-years away from Earth. That means the light we see from that star today left it more than a thousand years ago.

Astronomers call these strange worlds "super-puffs." Only a handful of super-puff planets are known in the entire universe, and finding two of them in the same star system is even rarer. "Their extremely low densities make them fascinating targets for understanding how planetary systems form and evolve," said Dr. George Dransfield, who led the study. In other words, these fluffy giants could help answer one of the biggest questions in science: how are planets born?

To feel how strange this is, try a thought experiment. The famous planet Saturn is so light that it would float in a giant bathtub of water. The super-puffs are far lighter still — closer to a beach ball than to a rock. They are giant in size but feather-light in weight, and nothing in our own solar system behaves this way. Each super-puff is like a natural laboratory, letting scientists test their ideas about planets against something truly alien.

The discovery started with volunteers — ordinary people, not professional astronomers. They were studying data from NASA's TESS space telescope, which watches thousands of stars for tiny changes in brightness. The volunteers noticed something strange: the star's light dipped a little bit, again and again, in a regular pattern. Those tiny dips were the first clue that planets might be passing in front of the star.

But a clue is not proof. To confirm the discovery, Dr. Dransfield's team used telescopes around the world. One observatory was especially important: ASTEP, located in Antarctica. In the Antarctic winter, the sun does not rise for months. That endless darkness let the team watch entire transits — the moments when a planet crosses in front of its star — from beginning to end. Each transit lasted more than 11 hours. These were the longest complete planetary transits ever observed from the ground.

Next, the team measured the planets. They figured out the sizes by studying how much starlight was blocked during each transit: a bigger planet blocks more light. Then they calculated the masses by watching how the two planets' gravity pulled on each other, slightly changing their orbits. Size plus mass gave them density — and the result was shocking. These Jupiter-sized worlds are lighter than cotton candy.

There was one more surprise. The two planets orbit their star in a perfect rhythm: for every five orbits the inner planet completes, the outer planet completes exactly three. Scientists think this musical pattern means the two planets were born together, from the same disk of gas and dust spinning around their young star. The findings were published in the scientific journal Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society on June 25, 2026.

And consider the distance: 1,113 light-years. Light is the fastest thing in the universe, yet even light needs 1,113 years to travel from that star to Earth. The fastest spacecraft humans have ever built would need tens of thousands of years to make the trip. These planets are distant neighbors — close enough to study with telescopes, forever too far to visit.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 안 보이는 행성을 찾는 법

How do you find a planet you cannot see? TOI-791 b and c are 1,113 light-years away, and they are far too dim to photograph directly next to their bright star. So astronomers use clever tricks instead.

The main trick is called the transit method. When a planet passes directly between its star and us, it blocks a tiny fraction of the star's light. It is like a fly passing in front of a street lamp far away — the lamp looks a tiny bit dimmer for a moment. By measuring these dips carefully, astronomers can tell that a planet is there, how big it is, and how long its year lasts.

NASA's TESS space telescope is a champion at this game. It stares at thousands of stars, waiting for dips. But TESS produces so much data that scientists cannot check it all alone. That is where the volunteers came in. Citizen scientists — students, teachers, and space fans — looked through the data and spotted the repeating dips around this star. Without their sharp eyes, these planets might still be unknown.

Seeing a dip is only step one. To be sure it is really a planet, astronomers must watch full transits from the ground. Here is the problem: each transit of TOI-791 b and c lasts more than 11 hours. At most observatories, the sun rises before the transit ends, and daylight ruins the measurement. It is like trying to watch a whole movie while someone keeps turning the lights on. Antarctica solved this. During the polar winter, the sun stays below the horizon for months, and ASTEP enjoyed weeks of unbroken darkness, so the team could record several complete transits from start to finish — the longest ever seen from Earth's surface.

From the transit, they learned the planets' sizes. The deeper the dip in starlight, the bigger the planet. Think of it this way: a basketball passing in front of a lamp blocks more light than a marble does. By measuring exactly how much light disappeared, the team could calculate each planet's width. Then came the harder part: weighing them. The team watched how the two planets tugged on each other with gravity, making each other's orbits arrive slightly early or late. From those tiny timing shifts, they calculated the masses. It is like guessing the weight of two dancers by watching how they pull each other as they spin.

Finally, density: mass divided by volume. The answer broke expectations. Despite being Jupiter-sized, these worlds weigh almost nothing for their size — less dense than cotton candy, a treat that is mostly air.

Why does density matter so much? Because it tells astronomers what a planet is made of without ever touching it. High density means rock and metal, like Earth. Low density means gas, like Jupiter. But an impossibly low density means something new entirely — a kind of world our theories never predicted, and a direct challenge to rethink them.

And then there is the rhythm. The inner planet circles its star five times for every three circles of the outer one. Astronomers call this a resonance, and it is a precious clue. It strongly suggests the two planets formed side by side from the same spinning disk of gas and dust, then locked into this dance billions of years ago and never let go.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 풀리지 않은 수수께끼

Now comes the biggest question: how can planets this big be this light? Nothing in our solar system looks like this. Jupiter is huge, but it is also heavy. The super-puffs break the rules — and scientists love a good rule-breaker, because it means there is something new to learn.

The leading theory sounds like science fiction. Scientists think each super-puff has a small, solid core — perhaps only a few times heavier than Earth — wrapped in an enormous blanket of hydrogen and helium gas. The thick atmosphere puffs the planet up to Jupiter size, while the total weight stays amazingly low. Picture a giant balloon with a marble hidden at its center: huge on the outside, almost nothing on the inside.

If this theory is right, it changes how we think planets grow. The classic story says a solid core slowly pulls in gas until it becomes a giant like Jupiter. But super-puffs seem to have grabbed far more gas than their tiny cores should be able to hold. How? Did they form farther from their star, where gas was plentiful, and then drift inward? Did something stop their cores from growing while the gas kept coming? Nobody knows yet.

This is why Dr. Dransfield's team is turning to the most powerful telescope ever built: the James Webb Space Telescope. Webb can split starlight that has passed through a planet's atmosphere and read which gases it contains — like tasting the air of another world from a thousand light-years away. Every gas leaves a unique fingerprint in the light, a pattern of colors that scientists can decode. If Webb finds thick hydrogen and helium with almost no heavier elements, the balloon theory wins. If it finds something unexpected — water vapor, carbon dioxide, or strange clouds — scientists go back to the drawing board, which is often when the most exciting discoveries happen.

The stakes go beyond two odd planets. For decades, the standard story of planet birth said that a rocky core must grow heavy enough — about ten times Earth's mass — before it can pull in a thick envelope of gas. Super-puffs seem to break that rule: huge envelopes of gas around cores that may be far smaller. Some scientists now wonder whether these planets formed much farther from their star, where gas was thick and cold, and then slowly drifted inward to where we see them today. Others think our whole recipe for making giant planets needs rewriting. Two fluffy worlds, and the textbooks start to shake.

Whatever Webb sees, the super-puffs have already taught us something important. Our solar system — with its rocky Earths and heavy Jupiters — is just one way to build planets. The universe is far more creative than we imagined. Every strange new world is a reminder: we are still beginners at understanding how planets are born.

And remember how this story started — with volunteers noticing tiny dips in starlight on a computer screen. The next great discovery might be waiting in data anyone can look at. Maybe even you.

There is a humbling lesson here too. Just ten years ago, most astronomers would have said such planets cannot exist — their gas should have escaped into space long ago. The universe keeps voting against our confidence. Every time we think we finally understand planets, a super-puff floats by and reminds us how much we still do not know.
