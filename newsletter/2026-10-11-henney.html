여객기보다 두 배 높이에서 사는 미생물들

2026-10-11 · One topic, three parts · English reading

Deep Reading

Part 1 — 뉴스 본문: 30킬로미터 위의 비밀 승객

Thirty kilometers above your head — twice as high as the tallest rain cloud — the sky turns black. Not the blue of a summer afternoon, but the pitch-black of outer space. You are so high that you can actually see the curve of the Earth beneath you. The air is so thin that it holds only one percent of the pressure at sea level. The temperature plunges to minus 60 degrees Celsius. Ultraviolet radiation pours down with almost nothing to stop it, powerful enough to damage the DNA of living things.

Nothing familiar could survive up there. And yet, this sky is full of life.

That is the astonishing finding reported by Science News Explores this week. For nearly two decades, a team led by microbiologist Brent Christner at the University of Florida has been sending balloons to the edge of space to ask a simple question: how high does life go? Their answer, published last year and making headlines again now, turned expectations upside down.

It started back in 2008. Christner was not really thinking about Earth at all — he was thinking about Mars. The surface of Mars is cold, nearly airless, and blasted by radiation. The stratosphere, the layer of our atmosphere between about 12 and 50 kilometers up, is surprisingly similar. So the team went hunting for the upper limit of Earth's habitable zone: the highest place where life can still hold on. Whatever they found would be a clue to whether anything could survive on the surface of another world.

Christner's graduate student Noelle Bryan launched sterile sampling balloons again and again, higher and higher — eventually to 38 kilometers, or 24 miles, up. Every single time, the collectors came back carrying living microbes. Not once did the team reach an altitude where they could find nothing alive. Life, it turns out, does not stop where the blue sky ends.

Part 2 — 더 깊이 알기: 평범한 미생물의 이중생활

The real shock was not that something survived up there. It was what survived.

Scientists expected extremophiles — the bizarre, exotic organisms that thrive in boiling hot springs, deep underground rocks, or pools of concentrated acid. Those creatures are built for the impossible. Instead, the stratosphere's passengers looked completely ordinary. They were the same everyday microbes that grow on farm crops, garden soil, and even human skin. Your neighborhood germs, drifting thirty kilometers above the Earth.

Think of it as a secret double life. The same microbe that lives quietly on a wheat field in one country can catch a ride on powerful high-altitude winds and settle down — weeks later — on another continent. At those heights, microbes travel at two to three times the altitude of commercial jetliners, which cruise at about 10 to 12 kilometers. From the cockpit of an airliner, a pilot would be flying beneath a sky that still holds thousands of tiny travelers far above the plane.

Christner describes the atmosphere as a highway system. Winds at altitude can carry microbes around the entire globe in a matter of weeks, letting them cross oceans and drop into brand-new habitats. Getting up there is surprisingly easy: storms, dust, and rising warm air can lift tiny particles skyward. Surviving the trip is the hard part — the burning ultraviolet light, the freezing cold, the near-vacuum. Many microbes manage it by going dormant, wrapping themselves in tough, seed-like coats called spores and simply waiting. A spore is life's pause button: metabolism nearly stopped, armor on, ready to wake up when conditions improve.

This redraws the map of the biosphere — the total zone of life on Earth. We used to draw it from the deep ocean floor to the highest mountaintops. Now its border reaches into the black sky above the weather.

Part 3 — 그 다음은: 질병, 화성, 그리고 외계 생명

Why does a sky full of microbes matter? For three reasons — and all of them reach far beyond the stratosphere.

First, disease. If ordinary microbes can ride high-altitude winds across oceans in weeks, then so can the ones that make people sick — and the ones that attack crops. Understanding this aerial highway could change how scientists track outbreaks and protect harvests. A germ that seems to appear out of nowhere in a new country might have simply dropped in from the sky.

Second, Mars. Remember why Christner started this work in 2008: the stratosphere is the closest thing to Mars on Earth. If common, everyday microbes can survive at 38 kilometers, then the surface of Mars — cold, thin-aired, radiation-blasted — looks less like a dead zone and more like a possibility. That cuts two ways. It is hopeful news for the search for life. But it is also a warning: when our spacecraft land on Mars, any microbes hitching a ride from Earth might survive the trip. Keeping other worlds clean — what scientists call planetary protection — just got harder.

Third, the search for life everywhere else. The same logic reaches further: the acidic clouds of Venus, the icy plumes spraying from distant moons, even the atmospheres of exoplanets light-years away. If life on Earth stretches far higher than we imagined, then the universe's habitable zones may be bigger than our textbooks say.

And the biggest questions are still open. Are these microbes actually living and dividing up there, or just sleeping through the ride? How exactly do they fall back to Earth — and what happens when they land somewhere new? The next balloon missions are being designed to find out. The sky, it turns out, is not empty. It is a highway, and traffic has been flowing for billions of years.
