The Last Lantern of Greyhaven

Chapter 3: The House That Knew His Name

Volume I — The City That Forgot the Sky

The rail cart stopped upside down.

This was not the sort of sentence Rowan had ever expected to think, but the evidence was difficult to dispute.

One moment the cart raced along a track beneath Greyhaven. The next it crossed a ring of blue light, the world twisted like a wet sheet, and Rowan found himself hanging from his safety strap while the houses of Saint Orra’s Fold stood neatly above his head.

Pip dangled beside him.

“I have identified a design flaw,” he said.

Mira released her strap, dropped upward, and landed on the ceiling—which had become the street.

“The design flaw is you,” she said.

Rowan followed less gracefully. His boots struck the stone, his knees folded, and the compass flew from his hand. It skittered toward the house with his name on the door.

The masked residents watched from windows and balconies.

There were dozens of them. Some wore coats from twelve years ago. Others wore patched uniforms or dresses made from curtain cloth. Their white masks had no mouths, only narrow eye-slits painted in silver. Nobody spoke.

Pip climbed out of the cart and bowed.

“Good evening,” he said. “Or morning. We have recently become uncertain.”

The nearest figure tilted its head.

Then every masked face turned toward Rowan.

He picked up the compass.

The needle pointed to the red letters on the house.

ROWAN VALE.

The paint was wet enough to shine.

“Someone knew we were coming,” Mira said.

“That is rarely comforting,” Rowan replied.

The house was narrow and crooked, built of dark green timber. Paper birds hung from strings beneath the eaves. They moved though there was no wind, turning their folded heads toward him as he approached.

On the threshold lay a pair of boots.

They were his size.

Rowan stopped.

Mira saw it too. “We do not have to go in.”

“Yes, we do.”

“That was not encouragement.”

“It worked anyway.”

The door opened before he touched it.

Warm air flowed out, carrying the scent of cedar, ink, and something sweetly familiar. Rowan stepped inside and was struck by a memory so sudden that it felt like a hand against his chest: being five years old, half asleep at a kitchen table, while a woman hummed and drew stars on the back of his hand.

He could not remember her face.

He had never been able to remember her face.

The room contained no furniture except a long table covered in glass jars. Inside each jar floated a tiny scene made of light.

A summer picnic.

A bridge under snow.

A woman dancing in socks while a kettle boiled over.

Rowan moved closer.

In one jar, a small boy sat on Silas’s shoulders and tried to catch falling ash. Beside them walked a woman in a red coat. Her face blurred whenever Rowan tried to look directly at it.

“My memories,” he whispered.

A voice answered from the stairs.

“Only the ones she could save.”

The speaker wore a white mask like the others, but hers was cracked across one cheek. She was tall and thin, with grey hair braided to her waist. In one hand she carried a lantern filled with blue flame.

Mira stepped in front of Rowan.

The woman looked at her. “Good. He needs someone who does that.”

“Who are you?” Rowan asked.

“Today?” She considered. “Senna will do.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only kind we have left.”

She removed her mask.

There was no face beneath it.

Not darkness. Not bone. Simply a smooth pale surface, unfinished as blank paper.

Pip sat down hard on the floor.

Senna replaced the mask.

“When the Fold was taken, the Gloam did not kill us,” she said. “It took the part of us that Greyhaven remembered. Names first. Faces later. We survived by storing what remained outside ourselves.”

She lifted the lantern.

Inside the blue flame, Rowan glimpsed a woman’s eyes.

“Your mother discovered how.”

The room seemed to narrow around him.

“Where is she?”

Senna’s masked face gave away nothing.

“Still walking.”

“Where?”

“On the road that isn’t there.”

Rowan clenched the compass. “Everyone speaks as if riddles are cheaper than sentences.”

“Riddles survive being stolen.”

A crash shook the house.

Dust fell from the beams. Outside, the masked residents scattered.

The paper birds under the eaves screamed.

They had no mouths, but they screamed anyway.

Senna extinguished her lantern with one sharp breath.

“The Blackwing has found the Fold.”

The darkness that had swallowed the seventh tower appeared beyond the upside-down rooftops. Its vast wings scraped against the lower bridges of Greyhaven. Pieces of shadow peeled from it and dropped toward the district, taking human shapes as they fell.

Gloamers.

Mira drew the short bridge knife she kept in her boot. “How do we get out?”

Senna pointed to the jars. “You take what she left.”

Rowan looked at the hundreds of captured memories.

“All of them?”

“Only yours.”

“How do I know which—”

Every jar on the table lit at once.

The scenes rose from the glass and circled him: birthdays, arguments, rainy walks, Silas laughing before grief had bent him, his mother’s hands folding maps into birds.

At the centre of the storm floated a memory he did not recognize.

Elian Vale stood in this room twelve years earlier, wearing the red coat. Her face remained blurred, but her voice was clear.

“If you are seeing this, Ro, the seventh light has failed.”

Rowan forgot the creatures outside.

His mother bent toward the memory, as though she could see him watching from the future.

“The lanterns do not hold Greyhaven above the Gloam. They hold something beneath it. The city was built as a lock, and our family drew the key.”

She placed a small brass object on the table.

It looked like half a star.

“You will be told that opening the sky is dangerous. They will be right. You will be told that keeping it closed is safer. They will be wrong.”

The memory flickered.

Behind her, Silas appeared, younger and furious.

“You cannot leave this to a child.”

“I am leaving him a choice.”

“You are leaving him a war.”

Elian turned away from Silas.

“Rowan, find the second half of the star at the Lamplighter Academy. Trust the girl who lies badly. Trust the boy who jokes when he is afraid. And when the compass points to NAME—”

The house lurched.

The memory shattered into sparks.

A Gloamer burst through the wall.

Mira moved first.

She threw her knife. The blade passed through the creature’s shoulder, trailing blue fire from a mineral lamp it had struck on the way. The Gloamer twisted toward her, wearing three stolen faces.

Pip’s brass arm snapped outward and closed around the knife handle.

“I knew stealing compass parts would become heroic,” he cried.

He pulled. The burning blade tore through the creature, opening a seam of light. Senna raised her lantern and poured blue flame into the wound.

The Gloamer came apart soundlessly.

More shadows climbed over the roofs.

Senna shoved the half-star into Rowan’s palm. It was warm, and tiny lines moved across its surface like roads seen from far above.

“Go to the academy.”

“What about these people?”

“They have been waiting twelve years. They can wait badly for one more night.”

The front door tore free and vanished into the dark.

Masked residents filled the street, carrying blue lanterns. Together they formed a line between the house and the approaching Gloamers.

Rowan hesitated.

One of them turned. Though its mask had no expression, Rowan understood.

Run.

Senna led them through the back of the house into a garden growing downward toward the mist. At its edge stood a small boat tied to nothing. Its sail was made of stitched maps.

Mira stared over the side. “There is no water.”

“Fortunately,” Senna said, “it is not a boat.”

The Blackwing struck the district.

Houses shuddered. Bridges cracked. Hundreds of paper birds rose into the air, burning blue at their edges.

Rowan, Mira, and Pip climbed aboard.

Senna cut the rope.

The boat fell sideways.

It sailed across open air, carried by a current Rowan could neither see nor understand. Behind them, Saint Orra’s Fold tilted deeper into the Gloam while lanterns flashed in the darkness like defiant stars.

Rowan held the half-star against his chest.

For twelve years he had wanted one thing: proof that his mother had not simply abandoned him.

Now he had it.

It did not feel like comfort.

It felt like a door opening beneath his feet.
