The Last Lantern of Greyhaven

Chapter 5: Lessons for the End of the World

Volume I — The City That Forgot the Sky

There are several disadvantages to discovering that your city is being used as a lid.

The first is that nobody sleeps.

The second is that adults begin holding meetings.

By midnight, the Lamplighter Academy had produced seventeen committees, nine emergency notices, and one argument about whether the end of Greyhaven required a revised bell schedule. Headmistress Rook solved the last problem by throwing the schedule into a furnace.

Rowan, Mira, and Pip were given beds in the first-year dormitory.

None of them used them.

The completed star floated above Rowan’s blanket, turning slowly and casting thin roads of light across the ceiling. Whenever he reached for it, the map of Greyhaven unfolded in the air: bridges, towers, hidden tunnels, and the seven chains descending beneath the city.

The broken chain moved.

Not much. A tremor. A tightening.

As if something below were testing how much freedom it had.

Rowan tried to remember his mother’s kitchen dance.

He found only facts.

Winter. Red socks. A foolish song.

The feeling had been cut away cleanly.

He hated the vault for taking it. He hated himself for offering it. Most of all he hated that the star had been worth the price.

Mira sat on the windowsill, sharpening her bridge knife with unnecessary violence. Pip occupied the floor, surrounded by parts borrowed from the academy under a definition of “borrowed” that no academy official would have recognized.

“You should sleep,” Mira said.

“So should you.”

“I am guarding the window.”

“From what?”

She looked at the darkness beyond the glass.

“Everything.”

Pip held up a copper coil. “Good news. I have invented a device that may preserve a memory outside the skull.”

Rowan sat up. “May?”

“The academy keeps using that word as if it were criticism.”

The device resembled a teapot being strangled by wire.

Mira stared. “What happens if it fails?”

“Depending on which part fails, we lose the memory, the table, or one eyebrow.”

“Whose eyebrow?”

Pip considered. “The nearest.”

Rowan laughed before he could stop himself.

The sound felt disloyal.

Then he remembered Silas standing alone on the station platform, and the laugh disappeared.

At dawn, Headmistress Rook summoned them to the Hall of Last Light.

It was not a hall. It was a vertical chamber through the centre of the clock tower. Platforms rotated around a column of blue fire. Fifty Lamplighter students stood at different levels, wearing training coats and expressions ranging from determination to the pale realization that education had become dangerous.

Rook addressed them from a moving staircase.

“The seventh chain is broken. The western lantern has failed. We estimate that the remaining locks may hold for three days.”

A hand rose.

“What happens after three days?” asked a small student with enormous spectacles.

“The estimates become less useful.”

Nobody asked again.

Rook explained that Gloamers were made from stolen memory. They could not be defeated by ordinary weapons because they had no stable shape. Lamplighters fought them using namefire: blue flame produced when a person spoke a truth strongly enough to give form to the world.

“To name a thing accurately,” Rook said, “is to remind it what it is.”

Each student was given a brass staff.

Rowan’s first task was to light a candle.

He failed.

“You are overthinking,” said Master Fen, a broad man with one mechanical eye.

“I am thinking the normal amount.”

“For you, that is overthinking.”

Rowan held the staff toward the wick.

“Candle,” he said.

Nothing happened.

“More truth.”

“It is definitely a candle.”

The wick remained unimpressed.

Across the platform, Mira’s candle burst into blue flame on her first attempt.

Pip’s exploded.

“I named it enthusiastically,” he explained.

Master Fen made Rowan close his eyes.

“What does the candle do?”

“Burns.”

“What does it mean?”

“It means someone wanted to see in the dark.”

The staff warmed.

A spark touched the wick.

Blue flame rose.

For the first time since the vault, Rowan felt something inside him settle. Not heal. Healing was a word adults used when they wanted pain to become obedient. But the emptiness gained an edge. A shape.

A name.

Loss.

The flame grew brighter.

Training continued through the day. They learned to anchor themselves by speaking their full names. They learned to draw protective circles with staffs. They learned that Pip should never be allowed near an unsupervised flame reservoir.

Rowan discovered he was good at unroads.

The star revealed paths ordinary maps could not show: steps through shadows, doors hidden in reflections, distances that folded when nobody looked directly at them. He crossed the Hall of Last Light without touching a bridge, stepping from the shadow of a bell into the reflection on Master Fen’s metal eye.

He emerged upside down and knocked over three instructors, but Rook called the result promising.

During the evening meal, Mira disappeared.

Rowan found her in the academy archive, standing before a cabinet marked ELIMINATED DISTRICTS. In her hand was an old envelope.

His name was written on it.

“You have had that all day?” he asked.

Mira turned too quickly.

“No.”

His mother’s compass clicked in his pocket.

The needle pointed to TRUTH.

“Mira.”

She closed her eyes.

“Six years.”

The archive seemed to become very quiet.

She gave him the letter.

The seal bore the same seven-flame symbol as the compass.

“My mother worked in the city records office,” Mira said. “After Saint Orra’s Fold vanished, she found this hidden inside a census book. It said I was to give it to you when the seventh lantern flickered.”

“You knew this might happen?”

“I knew someone believed it might.”

“And you said nothing.”

“I thought it was nonsense. Then, two years ago, the western light flickered for seven seconds. I started spending more time near your shop.”

Rowan stared at her.

Their friendship rearranged itself in his memory. Shared lunches. Arguments. Mira appearing whenever he explored somewhere forbidden. Mira learning every route to Vale & Son.

“Was any of it real?”

She flinched.

It was a small movement.

It hurt more than anger would have.

“I stayed because of the letter,” she said. “I kept staying because of you. I did not know how to explain the difference.”

His mother’s message returned to him.

Trust the girl who lies badly.

“She knew,” Rowan said.

“I am sorry.”

He opened the letter.

Inside was a single sheet.

Mira will believe that protecting you means keeping you from danger. She is wrong. Let her stand beside you instead.

Below it, in smaller writing:

Also, she cheats at cards.

Mira looked offended. “I do not cheat.”

The compass needle remained on TRUTH.

Rowan raised an eyebrow.

“Not often,” she muttered.

The city shook.

Every cabinet door flew open. Thousands of papers rushed into the air.

The clocks above them stopped counting backward.

For one heartbeat, there was silence.

Then they all struck zero.

The western wall of the archive darkened as if soaked with ink. A shape pressed through the stone from the other side: the outline of a vast beak, curved and sharp.

The Blackwing had not come for the lantern tower.

It had come for the star.

The wall burst inward.

Rowan pulled Mira down as shadow tore through the room. Shelves vanished. Papers became black birds. Somewhere in the tower, alarm bells screamed.

The star rose from beneath Rowan’s shirt and unfolded its map.

One road burned brighter than all the others.

It led straight through the Blackwing.

And on the far side of the creature, in letters of blue fire, the map wrote one word.

SILAS.
