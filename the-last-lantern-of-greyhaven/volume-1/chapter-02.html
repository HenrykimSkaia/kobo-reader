The Last Lantern of Greyhaven

Chapter 2: When the Seventh Lantern Went Out

Volume I — The City That Forgot the Sky

The darkness did not arrive like night.

Night, Rowan understood, was supposed to be gradual. It belonged to sunsets, bedtime, and the soft closing of curtains. This darkness fell across the western districts like a dropped curtain made of ink.

The seventh lantern tower vanished inside it.

For half a heartbeat, Greyhaven stood silent.

Then every bell in the city began to ring.

Market bells clanged from rooftops. Bridge alarms hammered beneath the streets. The fire towers added their shrill brass voices, though nothing was burning yet. People poured from shops and houses, staring west. Above them, the thing around the dead lantern opened its wings wider.

They were not wings made for flight.

They were made of absence.

Where they passed, chimneys, cables, and balconies seemed to disappear—not destroyed, but removed from the eye. Rowan could still hear metal groaning in the dark. He simply could not see what made the sound.

Silas seized him by the collar.

“Cellar. All of you.”

Rowan did not move. “The message was from her.”

“Cellar.”

“My mother knew this would happen.”

Silas’s grip tightened. “Which is precisely why you will do as I say.”

Outside, the street lamps went out one by one.

Not together. That would have been less frightening.

The darkness moved toward them, choosing each flame.

Mira grabbed Rowan’s sleeve. “Argue later.”

Pip had already opened the cellar door. “I would like the record to show that I was first to demonstrate survival instincts.”

“You tried to test lightning with a spoon last week,” Mira said.

“It was an insulated spoon.”

“It melted.”

“Only the metal part.”

A sound rolled over the city.

At first Rowan thought it was thunder. Then the rooftops shook, and something enormous struck the bridge three streets away.

The window burst inward.

Silas threw up an arm. Glass sprayed across the shop. A wind smelling of cold iron and old rain tore maps from the walls. Hundreds of pages lifted at once, flapping through the room like frightened birds.

In the street, people ran east.

Behind them came figures made of Gloam.

They were roughly human in shape, if a human had been remembered badly. Their arms were too long. Their heads bent at uncertain angles. Faces formed and vanished across them: a woman’s mouth, a child’s eye, an old man’s teeth. Wherever their fingers touched a wall, painted signs faded blank.

A baker stumbled near the corner. One of the creatures caught his shoulder.

He did not scream.

He simply stopped.

The creature leaned close, and silver mist flowed from his mouth into its own.

When it released him, the baker looked around in terror.

“What is my name?” he asked.

Rowan’s stomach turned cold.

“Gloamers,” Silas whispered.

Mira looked at him. “You said those were stories.”

“I lied.”

“You are improving.”

Silas dragged a heavy map cabinet away from the wall. Behind it stood a narrow iron door Rowan had never seen.

The compass in Silas’s hand pulled toward it so sharply that the chain snapped.

“Through here,” he said.

The door opened onto a tunnel sloping beneath the shop.

Pip peered inside. “Is this another thing you forgot to mention?”

“Yes.”

“How many are there?”

“If we survive, I’ll make you a list.”

They entered as something struck the front of the shop.

Shelves toppled. Wood split.

Silas slammed the iron door behind them and turned a wheel-lock until bolts shot into the stone.

The tunnel was barely wide enough for one person. Blue mineral lamps glimmered along the floor, though nobody had lit them. Rowan recognized the masonry from Silas’s oldest drawings: pre-Vanishing construction, built before the present bridge system.

The compass floated in Rowan’s thoughts.

HOME. TRUTH. NAME. SKY.

“Where does this go?” he asked.

“To Saint Orra’s Fold.”

Silas’s answer stopped all three children.

“That district is gone,” Mira said.

“Most of it.”

A violent tremor ran through the tunnel. Dust sifted from above.

Silas pushed them onward.

They descended beneath streets Rowan had known all his life. The bell noise faded. In its place came the deep creaking of Greyhaven’s chains and, farther below, a whispering sound like many people speaking from another room.

The Gloam.

Rowan had never been so close to it.

At the foot of the stairs, the tunnel opened onto an abandoned station. Rusted tracks curved into darkness. A small rail cart waited beneath a canvas cover.

Pip’s fear briefly lost a battle with delight.

“Oh,” he breathed. “I forgive everything.”

“You haven’t seen whether it works,” Mira said.

Pip pulled away the cover. “Neither has caution.”

The cart was no larger than a market wagon. Brass levers surrounded a padded bench. A glass sphere at the front contained a trembling blue flame.

Silas inserted the black compass into a socket in the control panel.

The engine woke with a cough.

“So,” Pip said reverently, “your mysterious family secrets include illegal transport.”

“They include many regrettable choices.”

“Can I inherit some?”

The station door above them exploded inward.

A Gloamer dropped onto the platform.

Its body changed as it moved. It wore fragments of faces taken from the street: the baker’s frightened eyes, a soldier’s jaw, the smile of someone Rowan had never met. It reached toward them with fingers that ended in drifting mist.

Silas shoved the children into the cart.

“Go.”

Mira grabbed his coat. “You too.”

“I have to release the brake bridge.”

“Then release it from inside.”

“It was designed by an idiot.”

Pip studied the levers. “Which idiot?”

“Me.”

The Gloamer crossed the platform without making a sound.

Silas pulled a short rod from his sleeve. With a twist, it extended into a staff of polished brass. Lines of blue fire raced along its length.

Rowan had never seen him hold a weapon.

He had never seen him stand like that.

For the first time, Silas Vale did not look like a tired mapmaker who disliked customers. He looked like a man who had spent twelve years pretending to be one.

“Take them east,” he told Rowan. “Do not stop until the compass points up.”

“I’m not leaving you.”

“You are not leaving me.” Silas gave him a strange, fierce smile. “You are carrying the map.”

The creature lunged.

Silas struck the platform with the staff.

Blue light burst outward. The Gloamer recoiled, faces rippling across its body.

At the same moment, the cart jerked forward.

Mira had found the throttle.

Rowan reached for Silas, but the platform was already moving away. Silas pulled a red lever beside the track. Metal screamed. The bridge behind the cart began to rise.

The last thing Rowan saw was Silas standing alone in the blue light, staff raised, while darkness gathered around him.

Then the tunnel curved.

The station disappeared.

The cart plunged downward.

For several minutes there was only wind, sparks, and Pip shouting advice to an engine that had not requested it. The tracks ran beneath Greyhaven, through stone shafts and across narrow bridges suspended above silver mist.

At last the tunnel opened.

The missing district lay before them.

Saint Orra’s Fold had not fallen.

It hung upside down beneath the city.

Houses clung to the underside of broken bridges. Gardens grew toward the Gloam. Windows glowed with blue candlelight. Streets curved along impossible angles, as though gravity had forgotten which way it preferred.

And above the inverted rooftops, hundreds of pale figures looked up at Rowan’s passing cart.

Every one of them wore a blank white mask.

The compass tore itself from the control panel and flew into Rowan’s hand.

The needle pointed toward a single house at the far end of the hanging district.

On its door, painted in fresh red ink, was Rowan’s name.
