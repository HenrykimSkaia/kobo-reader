The Last Lantern of Greyhaven

Chapter 1: The Boy Who Collected Lost Directions

Volume I — The City That Forgot the Sky

The city of Greyhaven had been built by people who did not trust the ground.

This was sensible, Rowan Vale had always thought, because nobody living could remember seeing the ground.

Far below the city, beneath seven miles of bridges, chains, chimneys, and dangling gardens, there was only the Gloam: a silver-black mist that swallowed dropped coins, broken tools, unlucky pigeons, and—according to Mrs. Brindle at the bakery—children who failed to return library books.

Rowan suspected the last part had been added for commercial reasons. Mrs. Brindle sold very good buns but possessed the soul of a tax collector.

Greyhaven floated above the Gloam on seven enormous lantern towers. Each tower burned with a flame as old as the city. Their light passed through glass ribs and brass mirrors, spreading a permanent amber dusk across the streets. The people of Greyhaven called this safety. Rowan called it disappointing.

He preferred maps.

Maps admitted that the world was larger than the road beneath your shoes.

On the morning everything began, Rowan was hanging upside down from the sign above Vale & Son, Cartographers, while trying to rescue a paper dragon from the gutter.

“It is not a dragon,” said Mira Quill from the pavement. “It is an overdue invoice with wings.”

“It has a noble profile.”

“It has the mayor’s seal on its tail.”

“Even dragons must pay taxes.”

Rowan stretched farther. His coat slipped over his head, blinding him. Below, Mira made the impatient clicking sound she reserved for badly tied knots, slow readers, and Rowan.

Mira was fourteen, exactly three months older than him, and treated this fact as a royal appointment. Her black hair was cut at the jaw because, as she often explained, long hair offered enemies an unnecessary handle. She had never identified these enemies. Rowan assumed they were mostly barbers.

The paper creature fluttered just beyond his fingertips.

A brass hand shot out of the second-floor window and snatched it.

“Mine,” announced Pip Noll.

Pip leaned out, grinning, with a pair of magnifying lenses strapped over his curls and half a mechanical arm attached to his sleeve. The brass fingers opened and closed with a squeak.

Rowan stared. “Did you build that from my father’s compass parts?”

“Only the parts he was not using.”

“He was using all of them.”

“Then he was using them very quietly.”

The shop door opened.

All three children became suddenly interested in architecture.

Silas Vale stood in the doorway, wearing his ink-stained waistcoat and the expression of a man who had just discovered a new continent and disliked it. He was not Rowan’s father, despite the name painted above the shop. The sign had belonged to Rowan’s grandfather, and Silas claimed changing it would confuse the customers.

Greyhaven’s customers were easily confused. Many still asked when Rowan’s grandfather would return, though he had been dead for eleven years.

“Inside,” Silas said.

They obeyed.

Vale & Son smelled of paper, sealing wax, dust, and the faint mineral tang of lamp oil. Maps covered every wall. Street plans curled from drawers. Bridge charts hung from hooks. Shipping routes, ventilation shafts, abandoned stairwells, rain collectors, bell tunnels—if something in Greyhaven went somewhere else, Silas had drawn it.

Except the sky.

There were no maps of the sky.

Rowan had once asked why.

Silas had answered, “Because maps describe places.”

This had not discouraged him.

On the worktable lay a parcel wrapped in oilcloth. It had arrived before dawn with no sender’s name. The string was wet, though there had been no rain for three weeks.

Mira reached toward it.

Silas slapped her hand away without looking. “Excellent instincts. Terrible judgment.”

Pip’s brass fingers extended.

Silas slapped those too.

“This,” Silas said, cutting the string, “was delivered through the old eastern chute.”

Rowan stopped smiling.

The eastern chute had been sealed since the Vanishing, the night twelve years ago when an entire neighborhood called Saint Orra’s Fold had disappeared into the Gloam. Not burned. Not collapsed. Disappeared. In the morning, its bridges ended in empty air, and thousands of people could no longer remember the names of those who had lived there.

Silas unfolded the cloth.

Inside lay a compass.

It was not the kind used by bridge engineers. Its case was black iron, etched with a circle of seven flames. Instead of north, south, east, and west, the face bore four words:

HOME.
TRUTH.
NAME.
SKY.

The needle spun once.

Then it pointed directly at Rowan.

Nobody spoke.

Pip finally whispered, “That seems rude.”

Silas closed the compass.

Too quickly.

Rowan had seen the change in his face—the brief tightening around the eyes, the way his thumb pressed the latch as if holding something inside.

“You know what it is,” Rowan said.

“No.”

“You are a terrible liar.”

“I am an excellent liar. You are simply an exhausting audience.”

Mira folded her arms. “Who sent it?”

Silas looked toward the window. Outside, the lantern light had dimmed as a cloud of soot passed between the towers.

“I don’t know.”

This lie was better.

Rowan felt it because it hurt.

There were subjects Silas avoided: Saint Orra’s Fold, the eastern chute, and Rowan’s mother, Elian Vale, who had vanished during the same winter. Silas never said she was dead. He never said she was alive. He spoke of her the way cartographers spoke of roads erased by landslides—once useful, now dangerous to follow.

A bell rang somewhere deep beneath the city.

One slow note.

The shop windows trembled.

Silas went pale.

Greyhaven had hundreds of bells. Market bells, bridge bells, fire bells, funeral bells. But this sound came from below all of them, from the ancient mechanisms in the roots of the lantern towers.

Mira looked up. “What bell is that?”

Silas crossed the room and locked the door.

“The Night Bell.”

“It is morning,” Pip said.

“That,” Silas replied, “is why we should be afraid.”

The compass clicked in his hand.

The lid opened by itself.

The needle no longer pointed at Rowan.

It pointed down.

A thin line of blue light spread across the face, and letters appeared beneath the glass in a handwriting Rowan knew from the dedication inside his oldest atlas.

His mother’s handwriting.

WHEN THE SEVENTH LIGHT FAILS, FOLLOW THE ROAD THAT ISN’T THERE.

For one impossible moment, Rowan forgot the bell.

Forgot the Gloam.

Forgot to breathe.

Then the western sky turned black.

Not dark. Black.

People screamed in the street.

Rowan ran to the window.

Across Greyhaven, six lantern towers burned as they always had, bright and golden in the smoke.

The seventh was dead.

And in the darkness around it, something enormous unfolded its wings.
