The Last Lantern of Greyhaven

Chapter 4: The Price of Remembering

Volume I — The City That Forgot the Sky

The map-sailed boat carried them through places that did not belong beside one another.

It crossed beneath Greyhaven’s lowest bridges, where rainwater hung in trembling globes. It slipped through a tunnel filled with sleeping statues. It passed a window in empty air, and beyond the glass Rowan saw a sunlit field under a blue sky.

He pressed his face to the pane.

Then the window was gone.

“You saw it too?” he asked.

Mira nodded.

Pip looked offended. “I was repairing the mast.”

“It is made of paper.”

“And yet it was broken.”

The boat rose at last through a well in the centre of Bellmarket and landed in a fountain with enough dignity to soak everyone equally.

Bellmarket was one of Greyhaven’s inner districts, safely east of the dead lantern. Its streets were crowded with refugees from the west. Families slept beneath striped awnings. Merchants had turned warehouses into shelters. The air smelled of wet wool, lamp smoke, and too many people trying not to panic.

No one paid much attention to three children climbing from a fountain in a flying boat.

Greyhaven had become difficult to impress.

The seventh tower remained dark. From Bellmarket it looked like a missing tooth in the city’s golden crown. Bridges leading west had been raised or cut. Soldiers guarded every crossing, though Rowan noticed that they stood with their backs to the darkness.

The Blackwing had disappeared.

This did not comfort anyone.

Senna had given them one final instruction before the boat carried her back toward the Fold: reach the Lamplighter Academy before the Night Bell rang again.

The academy stood inside Greyhaven’s central clock tower, a black stone building so tall that its upper floors vanished into smoke. Four clock faces watched the city with hands that never agreed. Rowan had passed it many times and assumed it contained clerks, gears, and people who enjoyed being disappointed in children.

He had been correct about two of those things.

At the entrance, a woman in a blue uniform blocked their way.

“No visitors.”

“We are not visitors,” Mira said.

The woman looked at their torn clothes, soot-streaked faces, and Pip’s sparking brass arm.

“Then you are a maintenance problem.”

Rowan showed her the half-star.

The woman stopped breathing.

Within minutes they were taken through six locked doors, two moving staircases, and a lift operated by a small man who appeared to be asleep even while answering questions. The academy opened around them in layers: classrooms built between turning gears, suspended walkways, libraries that rotated to follow the lantern light. Students in blue coats hurried past carrying flame jars, wind gauges, memory slates, and long hooked staffs.

Pip forgot to be exhausted.

“This,” he whispered, “is the most beautiful place I have ever intended to steal from.”

At the top of the tower they were brought before Headmistress Ysabet Rook.

She was a compact woman with silver spectacles and a braid wrapped around her head like a question mark. Her office contained no desk. Instead, hundreds of clocks hung from the walls, each ticking at a different speed.

She examined the half-star without touching it.

“Elian Vale was told to destroy this.”

“She did not,” Rowan said.

“Elian Vale had a gift for treating orders as opening suggestions.”

“You knew her?”

“I expelled her twice.”

Pip brightened. “Twice?”

“She returned through the chimney.”

Rowan nearly smiled, and the fact that he could do so made grief sting more sharply.

Rook’s expression softened by the smallest degree.

“Where is Silas?”

Rowan looked down.

The headmistress understood.

A clock on the wall stopped.

Only one, but in that room the silence was noticeable.

Rook sent food, blankets, and a healer. Then she asked them to tell the story from the beginning. Rowan spoke of the compass, the Night Bell, Saint Orra’s Fold, and the message left by his mother. Mira corrected him whenever fear made him careless. Pip improved several details until Rook threatened to place him in an honesty examination.

When they finished, the headmistress removed her spectacles.

“The Lamplighters were founded to maintain the seven towers,” she said. “That is what the city believes. Our actual purpose is to guard the locks beneath them.”

“What is locked?” Mira asked.

“If we knew, we would be guarding knowledge rather than ignorance.”

“That sounds inefficient,” Pip said.

“It is government.”

Rook turned to Rowan.

“The second half of your star is here. It has been hidden for twelve years. I cannot give it to you.”

“Why not?”

“Because your mother arranged the protection.”

Of course she had.

Rook led them down through the clock tower to a circular chamber lined with mirrors. At its centre stood a stone basin filled with blue liquid.

“The star is held inside the Memory Vault,” she said. “The vault admits only those willing to leave something behind.”

Rowan stared at the basin. “Money?”

Rook almost looked amused.

“A memory.”

The word passed through him like cold water.

“What kind?”

“One you value.”

Pip took a step backward. “I have very few valuable memories. Most involve pastry.”

“The vault does not care about usefulness. Only weight.”

Mira looked at Rowan. “We find another way.”

“There may not be time.”

“There is always another way. That is what maps are for.”

His mother’s compass pulled toward the basin.

The needle pointed to TRUTH.

Rook saw it. “The compass does not command you.”

“No,” Rowan said. “It only points.”

He dipped his hand into the blue liquid.

The chamber vanished.

He stood in the kitchen of Vale & Son, much smaller than he was now. Snow pressed white hands against the window. Silas slept at the table, his head on a stack of invoices.

Elian danced across the floor in red socks.

Her face remained blurred, but her laughter filled the room. She placed Rowan on her feet and waltzed with him between the chairs. He remembered the warmth of her hands. The ridiculous song she sang about a king who married a turnip. The way Silas opened one eye and pretended to be scandalized.

Rowan understood what the vault wanted.

Not a fact.

A home.

He pulled his hand away.

The memory clung to his fingers in blue threads.

“I cannot,” he whispered.

Mira took his wrist. “Then don’t.”

“But she left the star here.”

“She also left you the choice.”

The Night Bell rang.

One slow note.

Every mirror in the chamber cracked.

From somewhere inside the clock tower came a scream.

Rook swore with impressive precision.

The blue liquid in the basin rose like a hand and seized Rowan’s arm.

His memory tore free.

For a terrible instant he saw it floating above the water: red socks, winter light, Silas smiling, his mother’s hands.

Then the vault swallowed it.

The kitchen disappeared from his mind.

He knew it had happened. He knew he had once danced with his mother.

But the song was gone.

The warmth was gone.

The memory had become an empty room with a label on the door.

Rowan cried out and struck the basin.

It split down the middle.

Inside the stone lay the other half of the brass star.

The two pieces flew together.

Light burst through the chamber.

A complete star hovered above Rowan’s palm, rotating slowly. Lines unfolded from it, spreading across the air in a three-dimensional map of Greyhaven. Beneath the city, seven chains descended into the Gloam.

Six ended in locks.

The seventh was broken.

Something vast moved below it.

Rook stared at the map.

“No,” she said.

Across the mirrors, words appeared in Elian’s handwriting.

THE CITY IS NOT FLOATING.

IT IS BEING HELD UP.

Then every clock in the tower began counting backward.
